Spain and France Declare Emergencies as Wildfires Force Over 200,000 to Flee

EU activates civil protection mechanism as fires merge near Madrid and engulf French tourist areas, with officials split between climate attribution, arson investigations, and land-use explanations.

Spain declared its first-ever national wildfire emergency on July 24, 2026, as fires in the Madrid region and Ávila province exceeded firefighting capacity, while parallel blazes in southwestern France forced the total evacuation of the Cap Ferret peninsula and tens of thousands of residents from the Gironde and Landes departments [1][3][4][16]. Spanish Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska declared the emergency in the Community of Madrid and Ávila province, transferring operational command to the Military Emergency Unit, with more than 10,000 people initially evacuated from several municipalities [3]. Madrid regional emergency management chief Carlos Novillo stated that the wildfire near the capital was "at its peak and is currently beyond the capacity of firefighters to contain" [19][22][31]. Regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso described it as "the worst fire in the history of the Madrid region," calling the situation "a perfect storm of extremely high temperatures and relentless winds" [21][24][32]. Two fires west of Madrid converged despite the deployment of over 2,000 personnel and 10 aircraft, with some 40,000 people evacuated or confined in the Madrid region alone [22][28][31].

French Interior Minister Laurent Nunez reported that 40,000 people had been evacuated from the Cap Ferret area, with 80 homes burned — 50 completely destroyed — and between 1,000 and 1,500 firefighters deployed [12][16]. The fire in Saumos, north of Cap Ferret, was the largest so far this season in France and was threatening to cut off the only road out of the peninsula [22][31]. Regional fire chief Marc Vermeulen described propagation speeds of 800 to 1,000 hectares per hour in a suburban area, stating: "The fire is highly erratic and gaining very significant force. One of the main fronts is very close to a semi-urban area. We are defending house by house" [10][21]. President Emmanuel Macron ordered the military to mobilize as the fire moved east toward the Bordeaux metropolitan area [34][36]. Nunez described the fire as "a large, self-sustaining fire that is extremely difficult to bring under control" [36].

Both Spain and France activated the EU Civil Protection Mechanism to request cross-border aerial reinforcements [6][8][16]. Macron announced that France would receive two Croatian Canadair aircraft, two Portuguese Air Tractor planes, and two heavy-lift Black Hawk helicopters from the Czech Republic and Slovakia [6][10][18][25]. EU Crisis Commissioner Hadja Lahbib confirmed that the EU was deploying three water bomber aircraft following France's activation of the mechanism [32]. Spain requested four firefighting aircraft through the mechanism, and Greece agreed to send two Canadair CL-415 aircraft to the Toledo area [14][28][38][39]. European Commission spokesperson Anna-Kaisa Itkonen stated that the EU was monitoring the situation with great concern and had mobilized aircraft and Copernicus satellite imagery, adding: "We hope all hell doesn't break loose, but we are preparing for a serious situation" [35][40]. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez described "a dramatic situation, not only in various Spanish provinces but also in regions of neighbouring countries" and stated that the government had deployed every available resource [5][10][14][16][22].

Sánchez explicitly linked the fires to climate change, stating: "I've said it many times before, but the climate emergency kills. We're seeing that across Europe and we're seeing that in Spain" [32]. He urged other parties to agree on a state pact to tackle the changing climate and blamed climate change for the increase in major fires, noting Spain had already faced 22 major fires this year, four times more than the same period last year [32][48]. The World Weather Attribution group published a study linking drought blamed on human-fuelled climate change to the heightened wildfire threat, reporting that extreme heat is accelerating soil water evaporation and altering European drought dynamics [5][25]. UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell called for an accelerated transition to renewable energy as the key to protecting people from climate disasters [17]. Víctor Resco de Dios, professor of forestry engineering at the University of Lleida, warned that Europe could soon see "Dantesque scenarios like those seen in California or Australia, with fires or fire complexes reaching hundreds of thousands of hectares or even a million hectares" [10][32]. The far-right Vox party claimed that the wildfires are unrelated to the climate emergency, a position contradicting the climate-attribution framing from Spanish officials and scientists [32].

Other officials pointed to human-caused ignition rather than climate as the immediate cause of several fires. Spain's Guardia Civil arrested one man and investigated another in connection with the fire in Burgohondo, Ávila, for apparently ignoring a ban on heavy machinery use that created sparks [24]. The mayor of Le Porge, Martial Zaninetti, stated that the wildfire appeared to have been started by a machine clearing undergrowth [10]. In Calabria, Italy, local officials accused malicious individuals of arson, pointing to rags doused in flammable liquid tied to the tails of stray cats to spread fires [6]. Salvo Cocina, head of Sicilian Civil Protection, reported more than 350 fires on the island and said the fires had human causes, adding that firefighting had become "a social and public order problem" [30]. Wildfire analyst Raúl Quílez offered a structural explanation, arguing that the abandonment of farmlands — which traditionally provided buffer zones — has made rural areas more exposed to fires, creating "a perfect cocktail for this type of fires" [36]. An environmental analysis similarly argued that land-use changes, including monoculture plantations, are making European forests more vulnerable alongside climate change [46].

Evacuees across both countries described panic, fear, and uncertainty. José Cobos, evacuated from southwest of Madrid, said: "The smoke was incredibly thick. You couldn't breathe" [5][24][32]. Olivia Jarratt, evacuated from the Truc Vert campsite on Cap Ferret, recounted being woken by sirens and leaving tent and camping gear behind, noting that loudspeaker announcements were in French and she could not understand what was happening [21]. David Morton, an evacuee from England, described seeing "several fires in the distance as we drove" and said it was "different from seeing it on the television" [21]. Javier Martin, a local resident, said he had lived in the area for 36 years and had never seen anything like it [22][31]. Forest owner Jean Mazodier, 86, compared losing his forest to losing a child [24]. Lorenza Benítez, evacuated from Bustares in Guadalajara, said: "My Bustares, everything outside it, all of the nature... everything has burned... It's very hard, very painful" [48]. Local Red Cross coordinator Felipe Argomaniz stated that being with evacuees and keeping them company was what mattered most, as the ordeal had hit everyone hard [48].

French and Italian officials paid tribute to firefighters killed battling the blazes. Two firefighters died near Bordeaux airport, and a firefighter named Alessandro Marchi died in Sicily [30]. Italian Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi paid tribute to Marchi, and Nunez expressed gratitude and support to the families of the fallen firefighters near Bordeaux [30]. Sicilian regional president Renato Schifani wished a speedy recovery to a firefighter injured in the same operation [30]. The deadliest fire of the season, in Almería, resulted in thirteen fatalities [11][33].

Franck Chaumès, president of the Gironde branch of the Union of Hotel Trades and Industries, called the situation "a tourist disaster that we are experiencing once again" and said the union was working to secure financial aid [21]. An insurance industry analysis warned that most European wildfire damage remains uninsured, raising systemic reinsurance risk and highlighting a growing protection gap [47]. Spanish authorities themselves acknowledged that not enough has been done to prevent and contain recurring wildfires, with the emergency declaration reflecting a recognition that these fires are no longer a temporary emergency [23].

Turkish and Qatari outlets reported that the fires extend across a Mediterranean "belt of fire" affecting Tunisia and Algeria [20][45]. Italy dispatched a Canadair aircraft to support firefighting in Tunisia's Kef province, where fires had burned approximately 4,400 hectares since May — a 63% increase over the same period in 2025 [20]. Algerian Interior Minister Said Saaoud stated that all fires posing a danger to the population had been brought under control [45]. The World Health Organization reported a 57% increase in land burned by wildfires across the European Region over four years, with 2.2 million hectares destroyed in 2025 [17].

As of July 24, the Madrid regional government's central representative, Francisco Martin, said that dropping temperatures, absence of wind, and increased humidity had opened "a real opportunity" to advance in controlling the fire [33]. The Gironde prefect, Sophie Brocas, stated that the fire had reached "an unprecedented magnitude" and that significant national reinforcements would be received [36]. The Landes prefect, Gilles Clavreul, reported that the situation remained highly dynamic and unfavorable, with many new fire starts ahead of the main blaze [24]. France was preparing a military Airbus A400M transport aircraft for firefighting, though it would take ten days to be ready [42].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
