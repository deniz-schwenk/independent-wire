Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO in Board Reversal as US Blacklists Megaspeed and California Opens Probe

The unanimous vote came eleven days after Huang's resignation and six days after the board formally ruled out his return, with shares surging more than 30 percent.

Nvidia's board of directors unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang as president, CEO, and board director on November 23, reversing a decision made six days earlier to rule out his return [1][6][11][21]. The reinstatement, effective immediately, came eleven days after Huang's November 12 resignation over an extramarital affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom US intelligence suspects of contact with China's Ministry of State Security [4][9][19]. On the same day, the US Commerce Department blacklisted Megaspeed and its subsidiaries over the risk of diverting advanced computing products to unauthorized end uses [22][42]; days earlier, California Attorney General Rob Bonta had opened a probe into Nvidia tied to the Huang scandal [30].

The board said it had consulted senior US government officials and concluded Huang's return posed no national-security risk [3][10][25]. Board chair Harvey C. Jones, who had stated on November 17 that the board had no intention of reinstating Huang and that the CEO search would be unhurried [13][17][24], said the board now believes "Jensen is the right person to lead Nvidia" and that his reinstatement serves shareholders, employees, partners, and customers [14][21]. Nvidia shares jumped more than 30 percent in premarket trading, adding over $900 billion in market value [7][10][22], though some early premarket readings put the gain at roughly 25 to 30 percent [3][9][25]. The BBC quoted experts describing the reversal as "the biggest U-turn in economic history" [5].

President Donald Trump publicly urged the board to rehire Huang on Truth Social and told CNBC he "made the deal" to restore him [13][14]. Trump said the US government "has no reason to believe that Jensen's return to Nvidia poses any risk to National Security" and called Huang a "great patriot and national treasure" [8][22]. Vice President JD Vance played a key role in discussions between the White House, Huang, and Nvidia [14]. A petition signed by more than 180,000 people, including Elon Musk, called for Huang's return, and Sam Altman greeted the reinstatement with a post on X [6][14][15]. Reuters framed the reinstatement as the product of presidential intervention following an abrupt board reversal [28].

Critics called the reversal a capitulation to presidential and investor pressure that broke governance norms. Senator Elizabeth Warren described the reinstatement as "reckless" and said Huang still owes Congress answers about Nvidia's compliance with export restrictions [14]. Senator Chuck Schumer said Trump was "putting his own financial interests ... before our national security" and vowed that Democrats would investigate any wrongdoing [7][9]. James St. Aubin, chief investment officer at Ocean Park Asset Management, said the return "sends a troubling message about the state of corporate governance in America" [7]. Analyst Jack Gold called the reinstatement "a big mistake" because the investigation is still underway, saying it "amplifies the notion that big 'tech bros' don't need to play by the rules like the rest of us do" [9]. Steve Sosnick, chief strategist at Interactive Brokers, said Huang was "too big to be fired" [7]. NBC News reported bipartisan anger in Congress, with lawmakers vowing to press ahead with investigations [29].

The espionage and chip-diversion allegations that triggered Huang's resignation remain unresolved. US intelligence monitored Alice Huang after discovering her contact with China's Ministry of State Security from spring 2025 to fall 2026 [4]. Her business associate Kang Wei also had intelligence connections, and Megaspeed sought advanced Nvidia chips while some allegedly reached networks suspected of circumventing US export controls [4][9]. The New York Times reported that Alice Huang's relatives and business partners have extensive ties to Chinese military contractors [34]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio coordinated a spring memo circulating intelligence that Alice Huang was an agent of China's Ministry of State Security [23], and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Washington "needs answers" on Nvidia's export of sophisticated components to China [22]. Singapore charged five individuals and six companies in the Nvidia chip case, though Singapore Minister Josephine Teo said the investigation into Alice Huang is not because of espionage [33].

China rejected the allegations. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said "так звана китайська шпигунська історія - це лише безпідставне звинувачення і наклеп" (the so-called Chinese spy story is merely an unfounded accusation and slander) [23]. China denied the espionage allegations against Alice Huang [1][7][9][14][15][24].

Market analysts read the share-price surge as a confidence rebound tied to Huang's singular role. Sam North, a market analyst at eToro, said "Jensen Huang's return underlines how much of the AI trade still rests on one man" [14]. Matéis Mouflet of XTB France described Huang as the "Steve Jobs" of 2026 and said his return gave markets the pretext they awaited to rebound [22]. Jacob Bourne of Emarketer said the return could help restore investor confidence but raised questions about the enormous influence of certain leaders on the future of AI [22]. Korean semiconductor industry coverage framed the reinstatement as relief for Korean chipmakers and a boost to AI investment expectations [36], while Japan's industry minister Ryosei Akazawa said Tokyo would monitor the situation closely for its impact on Japanese companies [35]. Singapore's Prime Minister Lawrence Wong responded to the reinstatement by stressing that global AI governance cannot be decided by one country alone [32].

Analysts also argued the scandal leaves lasting damage regardless of Huang's return. Stacy Rasgon of Bernstein said Huang's tendency to surround himself with "compañías cuestionables" (questionable companies) and willingness to mislead the board remain reasons for concern [17]. El País framed the reinstatement as a "sainete" (farce) [13], and Russian business daily Vedomosti called the two-week reversal a costly farce that saw Nvidia fall out of the world's three largest companies by market cap before the reinstatement [38]. La Vanguardia reported that Huang used humiliation to motivate Nvidia employees [40]. Huang returned to Nvidia's Santa Clara headquarters on November 24, where employees lined balconies and cheered; he wore a navy jacket instead of his usual black leather one and promised to be a better CEO [18][20].

The independent investigation into whether Huang was targeted by foreign intelligence will continue, and Nvidia's board has established a new National Security and Export Control Committee [6][21][25]. Huang is scheduled to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3 [7], and bipartisan lawmakers have called for tighter restrictions on China's access to advanced Nvidia AI chips and a review of the reinstatement circumstances [31]. The Commerce Department announced a rule five days after Huang's resignation to prevent Chinese firms from renting access to advanced AI chips installed in data centers in other countries [17], and Congress is considering bills that would further tighten export restrictions [17]. A shareholder lawsuit filed after Huang's resignation continues to put pressure on Nvidia's board [41].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society
Non-state organizations representing collective interests (NGOs, human rights groups, trade unions, religious bodies).
government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.
industry
Private companies, trade associations, and commercial actors.
international_org
Multilateral bodies and their representatives (UN agencies, IMF, IAEA, Red Cross, regional alliances).
judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.
media
Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
military
Armed forces personnel, commanders, and defense ministries.
