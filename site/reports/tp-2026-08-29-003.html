Iran War Reaches Six-Month Mark as Gulf States Seek Off-Ramp and Republicans Question Trump's Strategy

A temporary Hormuz transit arrangement, Qatari-Iranian talks, and Republican dissent over war costs mark the six-month anniversary of a conflict with no clear victor.

The US-Iran war — which multiple regional and international sources frame as a US-Israel war on Iran — entered its sixth month with a temporary Strait of Hormuz transit arrangement, active Gulf-state mediation, and Republican criticism of President Donald Trump's strategy. US Central Command commander Brad Cooper announced that internationally recognized sea routes in the strait are free of Iranian sea mines, with a military task force guiding 20 to 30 tankers nightly through the southern channel [14]. However, overall commercial shipping through the strait has collapsed from approximately 100 vessels daily to around five to seven vessels, according to RFI and Al Jazeera [8][13]. Iran has shown renewed interest in negotiations, with mediation visits from Pakistan, Oman, and Qatar, though Trump stated the US does not want to speak to Iran [14]. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov confirmed Iran-Oman negotiations on reopening navigation, while opposing any transit fee [24]. Al Jazeera noted the war has reached the six-month mark with no sign of slowing down [17].

Analysts across multiple regions assess the conflict as a protracted war of attrition with no clear victor [1][7][20]. Reuters concluded the war has reached a costly stalemate, with Iran surviving the military campaign and betting time is on its side while the US shifted from airstrikes to economic warfare [20]. Chatham House analyst Sanam Vakil stated the war has accelerated pre-existing regional trends but started nothing new [1]. Royal United Services Institute analyst HA Hellyer assessed there is no chance of the Iranian government falling in the next six months, and that state collapse from economic pressure would take years [1]. Prothom Alo reported analysts broadly agree the war will not result in regime collapse or a clear Washington victory [7].

The Trump administration maintains its military objectives have been met and is waging an open-ended economic campaign to force Iran's capitulation. Axios traced the evolution of Trump's endgame from ambitious military objectives to a collapsed memorandum of understanding and finally to economic warfare [3]. White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt stated Operations Epic Fury and Economic Outcast were conducted to destroy Iran's military and economy [5]. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced Operation Economic Outcast as an economic onslaught against Iran's financial connections globally [12]. The US issued fresh Iran-related sanctions targeting a Hong Kong-based entity and an individual linked to Iran's Bank Melli [15].

Iranian officials reject the US framing and threaten retaliation. Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei warned that if the economic war continues, not a single drop of oil will be exported through the Strait of Hormuz or from anywhere in the Persian Gulf, and that Iran will respond like an earthquake [11]. President Masoud Pezeshkian stated Iran does not seek war but will not submit to bullying [4]. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated that putting diplomacy back on track depends on the US understanding that pressure does not work [8]. The Iranian Foreign Ministry framed US sanctions as a continuation of the war that violates the UN Charter and national sovereignty [5].

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres called on the US and Iran to resume negotiations, with his spokesperson Stephane Dujarric describing it as sad that the war has reached six months with talks paralyzed [5]. Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani urged a return to diplomacy, stating the language of force and threats is not a solution [22]. Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Baqer Qalibaf called for reducing tension during his meeting with the Qatari PM [22]. Pezeshkian called for resuming implementation of the memorandum of understanding and solving differences through dialogue [22].

Gulf states are reassessing their security architecture after Iranian strikes hit refineries in Saudi Arabia, gas facilities in Qatar, and infrastructure in the UAE [9]. Qatar University academic Abdullah al-Otaibi argued that regional countries increasingly recognize the region's problems must be solved through talks among regional countries, and that the unilateral US-Israeli war decision plunged the region into chaos against the will of Gulf states [9]. King's College London professor Andreas Krieg stated the US is withdrawing and Gulf states must build greater self-sufficiency [9]. A new Mecca Pact defense alliance between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkey has emerged, and Gulf states are pursuing alternative export pipelines bypassing Hormuz [9]. Iran has demanded co-founder status in the pact, with Rezaei insisting Iran join only as a co-founder able to draft foundational documents, while analyst Hassan Askari Rizvi believes these conditions would likely be unacceptable to the pact's other members [23].

Republican senators have criticized Trump's Iran strategy. Senator Bill Cassidy stated Iran is strengthened, the US is weakened along with its allies, 13 Americans have died, and the US has spent between 25 to 100 billion dollars on munitions while losing credible deterrence [18]. Senator John Kennedy demanded clear answers, comparing the situation to four heart attacks and a stroke [18]. Senator Ted Cruz opposed releasing frozen Iranian state assets, calling it an extraordinarily bad idea to pay billions to theocratic fanatics who want to kill Americans [18]. House Speaker Mike Johnson blocked a congressional vote, arguing the administration is working at full speed on a solution [18]. Congressional Republicans as a whole continue to support the war despite political risk and concerns about costs [21]. Democrats have criticized the administration for dragging out the war, warning Republicans will pay the price in midterm elections, as the war has cost at least 18 US troop lives and $100 billion in military spending [6]. Al Jazeera reported that Secretary of War Pete Hegseth told a Senate hearing the war had cost $37.5 billion officially, with internal Pentagon estimates reaching $80-100 billion [13].

The economic costs of the Hormuz disruption extend far beyond the Gulf. Italy has incurred nearly 12 billion euros in extra energy spending [30]. Uzbekistan assessed potential damage at $1-1.5 billion, or 0.7-1% of GDP [29]. African countries including Kenya, Nigeria, and Ethiopia face fuel shortages and price increases, with Nigeria's President Bola Tinubu ordering 100,000 gas conversion kits and Ethiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed urging citizens to use fuel with caution [31]. Japan is reviving its naphtha stockpile system and considering subsidies for non-Hormuz crude procurement [26]. Thailand faces supply chain disruptions and stagflation risk [33]. DNV analyst Sverre Alvik warned that prolonged high oil prices could trigger permanent oil demand destruction, accelerating the shift to electric vehicles [27]. War-risk shipping insurance premiums have reached between 3% and 10% of hull value [32], though the International Union of Marine Insurers stated marine insurance is part of the solution, not the problem [32].

China has emerged as the principal beneficiary of the mutual exhaustion between the US and Iran, according to analysts [16][25]. Oxford political scientist Karabekir Akkoyunlu argued that a regime change has occurred in Iran but in the opposite direction from what Washington wanted, with a more extreme hardliner clique now in power, and that China's involvement is necessary for any lasting peace process [16]. Israeli analysts are divided on the war's outcome. Former Israeli intelligence analyst Danny Citrinowicz described the war as a colossal failure of Israeli strategy, saying Israel achieved operational but not strategic goals [19]. Senior research fellow Liora Hendelman-Baavur warned of lasting hostility toward Israel and the United States if the war is remembered through civilian casualties [19]. Senior fellow Alex Grinberg dissented, arguing Israel achieved its strategic goals by denying Iran the strategic capacity to threaten Israel [19].

The civilian toll includes the Minab school bombing that killed 120 children [10][13]. A father described finding children's bodies: «وقتی وارد حیاط شدم، بچه‌ها تکه‌تکه شده بودند... یک سر اینجا، یک پا آنجا» (When I entered the courtyard, the children were in pieces... a head here, a leg there) [28]. A local resident stated the school site was a military base 10-12 years ago but now has no military installation [28]. An analysis in The Conversation argued the administration's rhetoric of denying quarter and threats to destroy civilian infrastructure demonstrate intent to violate the laws of war, though the ICC lacks jurisdiction because neither the US nor Iran is a party [10]. US Secretary of War Pete Hegseth stated there would be "no quarter, no mercy for our enemies," which is a prohibited order under the laws of war [10].

Regional spillover continues across multiple fronts. In Iraq, Iran-aligned political factions are divided over weapons consolidation, with Harakat al-Nujaba's Haider al-Lami insisting all armed forces be included and former Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki warning any internal clash would be a loss for all of Iraq [2]. Hezbollah leader Naim Qassem rejected the Lebanon-Israel framework agreement as illegitimate and humiliating, calling for its collapse [8]. Israeli Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich presented plans to settle an additional one million Jews in the Negev and Galilee [8].

The US plans to deploy the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier to the Middle East [8], and has taken the first concrete action against an Emirati branch of Egypt's Banque Misr for trading with Iran [8]. US officials assess whether leverage will soften Iran's positions [14], as Qatari-Iranian talks continue aimed at reviving the memorandum of understanding [22].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
