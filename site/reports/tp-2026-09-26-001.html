Iran Submits Seven-Day Plan to Reopen Strait of Hormuz as Trump Weighs Response

The proposal, conveyed through Qatari mediators at the UN General Assembly, conditions a phased reopening on US sanctions relief, a naval blockade lift, and a ceasefire extending to Lebanon.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi announced at the United Nations General Assembly that Tehran has conveyed a seven-day plan to the United States through Qatari mediators, offering to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and resume nuclear talks if Washington lifts its naval blockade, releases at least $12 billion in frozen assets, removes oil sanctions, and agrees to a cessation of hostilities on all fronts including Lebanon [1][5][11]. The plan, modeled on a June memorandum of understanding that subsequently collapsed, carries a compressed timetable that accounts describe differently: some quote Araghchi saying the strait would reopen at the end of the seventh day [1][10], while others report the US taking required steps within four to five days, the strait reopening on the sixth day, and final-deal negotiations beginning on the seventh [6][38]. Araghchi stated that "the choice now rests with the United States" and that implementation would begin immediately upon US acceptance [5][14].

The war began on February 28 with joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran and has lasted seven months, drawing in Hezbollah in Lebanon and Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia [6][9]. The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas passed before the conflict, now sees approximately 18 daily transits, a fraction of pre-war volumes [5][32].

Tehran presents the proposal as a concrete, ready-to-implement sequence built on conditions already accepted in the June memorandum. Araghchi told reporters that "the conditions we have asked the US to meet is nothing new, nothing more than what was already in the Islamabad agreement, which was signed by the US president" [19]. President Masoud Pezeshkian said the ceasefire process would begin the day the US accepts and that Iran would allow UN nuclear inspectors into the country [5]. Qatari Prime Minister Al Sani reported that indirect talks between Tehran and Washington in Doha had made progress [10]. China fully supported the proposal, according to Araghchi [9][16].

Washington's posture holds that the naval blockade and sanctions are squeezing Iran and that there is no rush given US control of the strait. A US official told the BBC that mediated talks were positive while saying Washington is "not in a rush" [5]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said a three-hour Qatar-mediated meeting produced no breakthrough [1][10]. Citing the Wall Street Journal, CGTN reported that Trump has rejected the proposal and told aides he expects to resume bombing after November's congressional elections [6]. Trump declared at the General Assembly that he is in no hurry despite high oil prices and threatened to "annihilate the Islamic Republic" absent an agreement [5][8][18]. US Central Command stated it had redirected 122 commercial vessels to enforce the blockade [23]. State Department spokesman Tommy Pigott said "it's not the United States that has stopped" diplomacy from happening [23].

Analysts and Israeli media read the accelerated timetable as deliberate leverage on fuel prices and war fatigue before the November midterms. Crisis Group's Ali Vaez described the proposal as "a smart move" attractive to Trump because it would end an unpopular war and lower energy prices while restoring conditions favorable to Iran [10][29]. Quincy Institute's Trita Parsi said that if Trump sees the deal as politically beneficial before the midterms, "perhaps we will see unprecedented flexibility" [8]. Israeli outlet Walla News framed the seven-day deadline as an Iranian effort to exploit Trump's political vulnerability, citing polling showing his approval at 32 percent [27]. Former US negotiator Dennis Ross put the odds of a pre-midterm deal at roughly 30 percent, saying "the blockade is what's really squeezing the Iranians" [7].

Within Iran, senior officials and military figures signaled that no real concessions would follow under pressure. A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Iran will show no flexibility on its nuclear program even if the US accepts the Hormuz proposal, and that the waterway will remain closed until all conditions are met [15][20]. IRGC Commander-in-Chief Ahmad Vahidi said "we are not afraid of war" [9], and senior military figure Rahim Safavi warned that a new front centered on the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab strait could open if Americans start a new war [9]. In Iran's Parliament, member Ebrahim Rezaei criticized Araghchi's meeting with the "aggressor enemy" as an illegal act, while Speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf described two rival currents — one speaking of war without a mechanism to end it, the other underrating national capabilities [29]. RUSI research fellow Urban Coningham said Iran, denied war reparations, will not back down because control of the strait is its only route to economic recovery [18][37].

An 80-country bloc at the UN, led by Bahrain, called for the immediate and full reopening of the Strait of Hormuz as a matter of international law and freedom of navigation. Bahraini Foreign Minister Abdullatif bin Rashid al-Zayani read a joint statement demanding "the full restoration of navigational rights and principles without delay" [7][21]. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres met with Pezeshkian and stressed the need to resolve the conflict through dialogue with full respect for international law including navigational rights [4]. German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul called on Iran to end the blockade, rejected tolls including voluntary ones, and said Germany and the E3 stand ready to support mediation [35]. UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband met Araghchi in New York and made clear the UK is committed to freedom of navigation in the strait [9].

Market and risk-intelligence reporting indicates the strait remains effectively closed and dangerous regardless of diplomatic claims. War-risk premiums have reached 7.5 to 10 percent of hull value, roughly thirty to forty times pre-crisis levels, contradicting White House claims that escorts have kept Hormuz under control [25]. The Lloyd's Market Association stated that "the reason ships are not moving is not through a lack of insurance; it is a question of the risk to crew and vessel safety being assessed by the ship masters and owners as too high" [25]. An attack on the bulk carrier Cape Dao near the strait killed an Indian seafarer, prompting Indian seafarer unions to demand accountability and protective measures [26].

Gulf capitals are pushing back against the bilateral character of the Oman-Iran navigation arrangement. Saudi Arabia is concerned that the new shipping route deal is between Iran and Oman and not signed jointly with the Gulf states [19], and pulled back from endorsing the route, partly angered by Houthi attacks on Saudi territory [9]. Oman postponed a planned navigation meeting in Salalah, with Omani Foreign Minister Badr al-Busaidi saying the postponement stemmed from a desire for consensus among participating states [36].

One US ally is declining to join the Hormuz military effort. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung ruled out deploying troops or military assets to the strait, backed by survey data showing 52 percent of South Koreans oppose deployment [17]. Trump responded by announcing a substantial reduction of US-South Korea joint military exercises, linking the decision to Seoul's refusal to support the war [17].

Trump has rejected Iran's seven-day proposal and told aides he expects to resume bombing after November's congressional elections, according to the Wall Street Journal as reported by CGTN [6]. Araghchi said Iran awaits a formal US response [3], while Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said further contacts through intermediaries could be held in the coming days [1].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.

Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.

Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
