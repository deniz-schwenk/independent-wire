Trump Signals Readiness for Renewed Iran War Amid Chemical Weapons Allegations and Hormuz Crisis

The US president says he would 'absolutely' pursue war again; the IEA calls the Middle East conflict the largest threat to energy security in history.

President Donald Trump told Fox News he would "absolutely" repeat his decision to start the war on Iran, saying the US had concluded Iran "can't be trusted" [27]. The statement follows his earlier remark to Axios that he was in no hurry to decide on next steps and would see what happened after the start of the new year [15][32]. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is pressing Washington to join a four-to-seven-day Israeli air campaign against Iran, citing intelligence that Tehran is rebuilding missile and drone stockpiles and advancing chemical and biological weapons work [12][28][32], though analysts note previous Israeli intelligence claims about Iran have often proven false [12]. Mossad director Roman Gofman travelled to Washington to convey Israeli intelligence on Iranian chemical weapons research and argue for resuming military operations [15][32].

Trump accused Iran of "looking at gases, looking at bio" without providing evidence, and threatened that if Iran attacked US officials, "we wipe them out" [16]. US officials said there are signs Iran is developing chemical and biological weapons [26]. At the UN Security Council on 13 November, GCC states blamed Iran for attacks on neighbours and shipping disruption, with the GCC secretary-general saying member states have faced more than 7,000 Iranian missiles and drones since 28 February [31]. The Bahraini ambassador, speaking for the GCC, said 1,600 vessels were disrupted and 23,000 seafarers stranded, insisting "the security of civilians cannot constitute a bargaining chip or a tool for extortion" [31]. US Ambassador Jennifer Locetta accused Iran of attacking its neighbours, firing on commercial ships, and defying the Security Council [31].

Iranian officials reject the chemical and biological weapons allegations as shifting pretexts for military action. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baqaei called the claims a "big lie," saying "the accusations change but follow the same narrative pattern," and invoked Colin Powell's 2003 UN presentation on Iraqi WMD [26][2]. Independent Western experts find the evidence vague. Dan Kaszeta of the Royal United Services Institute said the question of whether Iran has a chemical or biological weapons programme "has been debated for decades, ever since the Iran-Iraq war" [16]. Jacek Siewiera of the Atlantic Council said "a research programme does not, by itself, establish the ability to carry out a particular operation abroad" [16]. Glen Rangwala of the University of Cambridge said "there is a fairly extensive history of successive US administrations making unfounded allegations about countries of the Middle East possessing weapons of mass destruction" [16]. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin both said there is no imminent threat from Iran [16].

Iranian officials and allied analysts frame the war as unprovoked American-Israeli aggression. Iran's ambassador to the UN argued the root cause is US and Israeli aggression and that Western members are reversing the roles of victim and aggressor [31]. Iranian Chief of Staff Amir Hatami warned that if the US repeats its actions, Iran is ready to confront it and its supporters "more strongly, even more crushingly, without geographic limits" [24]. The Iranian Foreign Ministry warned neighbouring states not to let the US use their territory for attacks, calling recent Iranian strikes on US Patriot systems at regional bases "legitimate self-defense" [25]. At the IAEA Board of Governors, Iran's Vienna mission accused Washington of double standards for bombing safeguarded nuclear facilities while demanding transparency, and an Iran-China-Russia joint statement tied the resumption of IAEA verification to guarantees against renewed attacks [20]. Analyst Kayhan Barzegar argued Iran blunted US and Israeli objectives using affordable drones and missiles, strengthening Tehran's bargaining position, and that the Strait of Hormuz has become a strategic lever Iran does not want returned to its pre-war status [13].

The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook calls the Middle East crisis the largest threat to energy security in history, dwarfing the 1970s oil shocks [4][10]. IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol said there is "no credible pathway for the energy world to return to its pre-crisis state" [4][10]. The IEA projects Hormuz flows falling from about 20 million barrels per day before the war to 8.5 million by 2030 and under 2 million by 2035 [10]. International Maritime Organization Secretary-General Arsenio Dominguez stated "it is just not safe to go through the Strait of Hormuz" [30]. UK Maritime Trade Operations data shows vessel crossings about 65 percent below pre-war levels [29], and at least 11 tankers were damaged in the strait in the first half of November, including a VLCC struck by a projectile that injured four crew [42]. The German Shipowners' Association's Martin Kröger said civilian ships cannot defend themselves against drones and missiles [43].

Some importing economies describe managing the disruption through diversification and reserves. China National Petroleum Corporation reported its Middle East project output has recovered to about 90 percent of pre-war levels, framing Hormuz disruption as a risk managed through diversified production [37]. South Korean refiners secured enough crude for September-October but face a November crunch as alternative cargoes mean longer voyages and higher costs [40]. India absorbed the shock by diversifying away from Hormuz dependence, cutting excise, and drawing on reserves [41]. African analysts say the continent absorbed the shock through refined-fuel prices, fertiliser costs, freight and fiscal channels rather than crude supply [33].

The US war in Iran has cost $48.3 billion as of 13 November, covering military operations, equipment and munitions but not damage to US military installations, Pentagon budgetary official Arthur Miller told the Senate Armed Services Committee [5][7]. Senator Elizabeth Warren cited estimates that the naval blockade costs about $32.5 million per day and that an Iranian attack on the US Navy's regional headquarters in Bahrain caused at least $400 million in damage [7]. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told sailors to prepare for deployments of at least 11 months and said at least 17 destroyers are deployed to enforce the blockade [22]. Analyst Omer Ozkizilcik of the Atlantic Council said "no tactical victory can make up for strategic mistakes" [22], and Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group warned that "without a realistic political objective and a credible diplomatic exit strategy, renewed military action risks prolonging the conflict rather than resolving it" [22]. Rubio said military strikes are unnecessary if economic pressure achieves the same objective [11][22].

On the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin held their first trilateral meeting, with Trump saying the three nuclear powers are "uniquely positioned" to reduce arsenals and vowing Iran will never get a nuclear weapon [18][8]. The joint statement pledged higher refined petroleum output and stable energy flows but omitted both Ukraine and Iran [38][23]. Analyst Ryan Hass of the Brookings Institution said the meeting recognises Putin "as a decisive player rather than a pariah" without any concession on Ukraine [14]. Hal Brands of Johns Hopkins University said the meeting could only happen if Beijing had received guarantees it would be "entirely free of substantive content" [14]. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte defended dialogue among nuclear powers as normal [21].

Gulf states are pursuing mediation. Gulf leaders asked Trump not to attack Tehran, according to a source involved in US-Iran diplomacy cited by Reuters [34]. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi briefed Oman's Sultan on a diplomatic initiative and discussed US-Iran proposals with a Qatari delegation in Muscat [34]. Qatar's foreign ministry spokesperson called on all parties to exercise restraint [35]. Analyst Trita Parsi described a possible "Swiss cheese" arrangement in which Washington quietly reduces the blockade's intensity while Iran gradually allows more tankers through Hormuz [6]. The leaders of France, the UK, Germany, and Italy welcomed the US-Iran memorandum and pledged a strictly defensive maritime mission including demining [39].

Inside Iran, ordinary citizens describe the blockade's impact on daily life. A 60-year-old truck driver from Kermanshah said Bandar Abbas now dispatches 20-30 loads a day versus 5,000-6,000 before, and predicted famine if the blockade continues [36]. A 40-year-old car oil-change shop owner in Tehran said business has collapsed to one customer in two weeks and that his family eats more bread and buys meat and fruit far less often [36]. A 46-year-old shoe seller said hemophilia medicine is nearly unavailable nationwide and warned that continued blockade means unrest [36].

Trump has told Vice President JD Vance to drop the pursuit of a negotiated settlement [12], while the White House has asked the Pentagon to assess Iranian responses to prepared strike options including the Pickaxe Mountain nuclear site, bridges, power plants, and Iranian military and civilian leadership [15]. A third US carrier strike group, led by the USS Theodore Roosevelt, has arrived in the Middle East [11].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.

judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.

Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
