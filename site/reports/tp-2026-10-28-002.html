US Warns Arab States of Post-Election Strike on Iran as Oman Blames Washington for Diplomatic Stall

IAEA chief demands inspector access to Iran's nuclear material while regional mediators race to avert renewed conflict before Israeli and US elections.

The United States has warned several Gulf states to prepare for a possible short and intense military operation against Iran after the November midterms, with US forces bolstering missile defence, restocking munitions and preparing bombers and fighter jets, according to a Wall Street Journal report relayed by Iranian media [6]. The warning came as Oman's Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi told a Cambridge University audience that "issues such as the strait of Hormuz have become impossible to resolve because external powers continue to define the terms of engagement" [8], and IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi told the UN Security Council that Iran has denied inspectors access to verify previously declared nuclear material, including 440kg of highly enriched uranium, since inspectors were withdrawn in June 2025 [18][20].

President Donald Trump has publicly framed Iran as the aggressor. At a rally in Corinth, Mississippi, Trump said "Iran started the war with us" and dismissed the April Pakistan-brokered ceasefire as "a waste of time" [10][13][16]. He has stated on social media that there will be no deal with Iran short of unconditional surrender [22], and has threatened further strikes on Iranian power plants, bridges and civilian infrastructure [12]. Israeli Defence Minister Israel Katz has repeatedly said he wants to send Iran "back into the Stone Age" [12]. Washington separately warned Iraqi officials that US forces would strike Iran-aligned armed factions directly and without prior coordination if they join a renewed conflict [2].

Iranian officials have set preconditions for any negotiations. MP Aladdin Boroujerdi said Iran will not negotiate until seven conditions are met, that its missile stockpile has grown by more than 50 percent and could sustain two years of war, and that it will not hand over its enriched uranium [17]. Iran's Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsen Rezaei said Hormuz will reopen and talks begin only after Washington suspends sanctions, unfreezes assets, lifts the naval blockade and guarantees no further strikes [1]. Analyst Sina Toossi told Al Jazeera that "Iran believes previous negotiations and ceasefire arrangements failed to protect it from renewed military attacks, and it is therefore seeking more tangible and durable guarantees" [1].

Oman's Albusaidi called for a non-aggression pact among the six Gulf states, Iran and Iraq, arguing that "any Gulf security architecture that doesn't include [Iran] is inherently unstable" [9]. The speech coincided with a Tehran visit by the defence and intelligence chiefs of Turkiye, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia — the Mecca Defence Alliance states — aimed at preventing renewed US-Iran fighting [8][21]. Qatar's Foreign Ministry described a separate delegation's visit to Tehran as part of ongoing mediation in which proposals continue to be exchanged between Washington and Tehran [3]. Saudi Defence Minister Khalid bin Salman warned Iran against further strikes while signalling Riyadh could activate its joint defence agreement with Pakistan [22]. Analyst Rami Khouri said the Tehran meeting aims first at de-escalation and a permanent ceasefire with stronger mediation by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye [1].

The IAEA's Grossi told the Security Council that inspectors have been available to return for months but Iran has denied access, stressing that any solution must include IAEA verification [18]. He said there is no indication Iran has attempted to repair or reconstruct nuclear facilities damaged in the military strikes [19]. Pakistan's acting UN ambassador Usman Jadoon warned that "the situation regarding the Iran nuclear file has sharply deteriorated" and urged that IAEA technical questions be handled without politicisation [4]. UN Secretary-General António Guterres, speaking in Cairo, said "we need maximum restraint and diplomatic solutions" and insisted navigational rights be restored in the Strait of Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb [11]. Brazil's foreign ministry condemned military offensives while expressing solidarity with Gulf states hit by Iranian retaliation, calling diplomacy the only viable path [29].

Israeli and US electoral calendars are shaping the timing of potential strikes. Israeli media reported that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu pressed the military to provoke a war with Iran before Tuesday's Israeli election, with Channel 12 reporting a clash between Netanyahu and IDF chief of staff Eyal Zamir over a directive that could spark an immediate conflagration [15]. Opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot said "we won't allow the IDF to be turned into a pawn in Netanyahu's survival campaign" [15]. US-affairs analyst Amir Ali Abolfath argued that a Democratic midterm victory would constrain but not stop a war because the president retains commander-in-chief authority, and that a strike before the 3 November vote is plausible if Washington believes it can force a surrender in days [14]. Trump's approval on the war stands at 34 percent, and Iranian officials are reportedly prolonging talks to deny him a pre-election win [1].

Russia's UN ambassador Vassily Nebenzia condemned "the aggression by the United States and Israel against Iran" as the root cause of the crisis and voted against a Gulf-sponsored maritime security resolution [27]. China announced it will send an envoy to countries in the region to help prevent another round of escalation [1].

The economic fallout is spreading across import-dependent economies. The UN Economic Commission for Latin America projected 2026 oil prices 20-25 percent above 2025 levels, with lasting impacts even if diplomatic progress holds [28]. India, which sources roughly half its oil through the Strait of Hormuz, faces direct exposure to price rises [30]. African economies including Kenya, Nigeria and Ethiopia have taken emergency measures over fuel supplies [31]. Gulf states have suffered over $120 billion in wiped market capitalisation in Dubai and Abu Dhabi and 18,400 cancelled flights [9]. Kuwait banned food exports and froze retail prices at pre-war levels [23]. Analysts say China's strategic and commercial reserves could cover up to six months even if Middle East supply were cut off, while Japan's energy agency cites about eight months of oil reserves plus roughly a year of LNG inventory [26][32].

Inside Iran, residents describe paralyzed ports and collapsing imports. A shipping specialist in Tehran told Euronews that "since the war began, maybe one out of ten cargo ships leaving China actually reaches Iran" [24]. A truck driver from Kermanshah said "Bandar Abbas is dead" [24]. A user on X, quoted by Al Jazeera, wrote that "90 million people have our eyes to the sky to see when there will be war" [12]. Another Tehran resident said Iranians who initially hoped for the government's overthrow were disillusioned after US and Israeli bombing of infrastructure "that we need to live, like oil and gas, power and steel" [12].

Qatari mediators spent Sunday in Tehran seeking terms under which Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz [8]. The Pentagon has continued briefing on a major US military buildup in preparation for a possible assault after the midterm elections [8]. Trump has said he has not made a final decision, asking publicly: "My question is, do I make a deal with them or do I finish them off?" [1].

framing
English-language and international-organization sources frame the nuclear file around Iran's denied inspector access and unmet verification obligations, while Persian-language coverage frames enriched uranium as a sovereignty red line that Tehran will not transfer under any deal.
Partially resolved: The corrected article now carries both sides: Grossi's and Jadoon's verification and de-politicisation demands alongside Boroujerdi's ruling out any transfer of enriched uranium. The underlying dispute over the uranium's fate is named but not resolved by the sources.
framing
US statements cast Iran as the aggressor that started the war and dismiss the ceasefire as wasted time, while Russia's UN ambassador names US-Israeli aggression against Iran as the root cause of the crisis.
Unresolved: The article presents both framings with attribution — Trump's rally claims and social-media ultimatum versus Nebenzia's Security Council condemnation — without adjudicating the war's origins.
emphasis
English-language and UN sources treat the Strait of Hormuz as a global navigational and energy emergency demanding restored navigational rights, while Persian-language sources present it as Iran's reciprocal leverage that will not revert to its previous state unless sanctions, the blockade and asset freezes are lifted.
Partially resolved: The article carries Guterres's demand for restored navigational rights and Iran's preconditions for reopening the strait; Boroujerdi's specific warning that Hormuz will not return to its previous status is not included, but the leverage framing appears through Rezaei's and Boroujerdi's stated conditions.
emphasis
Chinese and Japanese sources present the energy shock as a manageable problem absorbed through months of strategic reserves and import diversification, while Latin American and African sources emphasise inflation, fiscal strain and emergency fuel measures in import-dependent economies.
Partially resolved: The corrected article reports both the reserve cushions (now correctly attributed — analyst estimate for China, official agency figures for Japan) and the emergency measures and price projections elsewhere; the differing assessed depth of impact by region is not reconciled.
omission
Persian-language coverage quantifies the blockade's humanitarian toll on Iranian civilians — over 80 percent disruption of heavy imports and non-oil exports, near-70 percent inflation, and analyst assessments that food reserves cover several months — figures that appear nowhere in the article.
Partially resolved: The article includes resident testimony on paralyzed ports, collapsing imports and disillusionment, but omits the sources' quantified import-disruption and inflation figures and the World Food Programme's assessment that overland routes cannot meet Iran's needs long term.

Borderline formulations

Defensible readings exist on both sides

framed loaded_term (borderline 2/2)
'Framed Iran as the aggressor' is the article's own verb choice, and it can be read as subtly signaling that Trump's attribution is a narrative construct rather than a claim. It can equally be read as a neutral discourse verb accurately describing how Trump presents the conflict, which the article reports without endorsing either side.
Iranian retaliation loaded_term (borderline 1/2 · cleared 1/2)
'Gulf states hit by Iranian retaliation' can be read as the article summarizing Brazil's statement, but the word 'retaliation' is the article's own characterization of Iran's attacks, implying they were justified responses to prior strikes. It can equally be read as factually descriptive, since the article documents that US and Israeli strikes preceded Iran's attacks.
Analysts say hedging (borderline 1/2 · cleared 1/2)
'Analysts say' introduces a specific, quantified estimate without naming the analysts, while the parallel clause names Japan's energy agency. It can be read as legitimate attribution of an inherently predictive estimate, but also as anonymous sourcing the article could have specified — especially given it names analysts elsewhere.

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society
Non-state organizations representing collective interests (NGOs, human rights groups, trade unions, religious bodies).
government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.
industry
Private companies, trade associations, and commercial actors.
international_org
Multilateral bodies and their representatives (UN agencies, IMF, IAEA, Red Cross, regional alliances).
judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.
media
Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
military
Armed forces personnel, commanders, and defense ministries.
