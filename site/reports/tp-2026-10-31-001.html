Binance reports $6.3bn crypto heist, the largest in history

North Korea-linked TraderTraitor blamed by blockchain analysts as Binance pledges to cover all losses; regulators and security researchers debate systemic risks.

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, reported that approximately $6.3 billion in digital assets were stolen in a cyberattack on October 29, 2026, with most funds moved out of Binance wallets across ten blockchains in roughly 20 minutes [2][4][11]. The exchange suspended all deposits and withdrawals, stated that user funds were safe, and pledged to cover all losses from its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and company resources [2][5][28]. Binance co-CEO Richard Teng described the incident as a complex, multi-vector attack that began with a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop but later uncovered additional breach points, insisting no wallet private keys were leaked and that the drained funds were held in Binance's own hot wallet rather than customer accounts [5][29][20]. The exchange said it had set aside more than $6.34 billion, including $5 billion in the exact affected token assets, to cover the stolen assets [20][28].

Blockchain analytics firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis attributed the attack with high confidence to North Korea's TraderTraitor unit, also known as the Lazarus Group, citing on- and off-chain intelligence and similarities to the February 2025 Bybit hack [1][2][26][39]. TRM Labs' global head of policy, Ari Redbord, stated that when North Korea steals at this scale, the money funds weapons proliferation and other destabilizing activity, framing the hack as a global security issue rather than just a crypto theft [2][24]. Elliptic chief scientist Tom Robinson called the Binance theft "almost certainly the largest theft of any kind in history" [12]. However, ISTEC Business School researcher Loïc Sauce cautioned that blockchain tracking supports strong suspicions toward a North Korea-linked group but that no official confirmation has been issued, and Binance itself declined to confirm the link pending its investigation [22][24].

The attack's mechanics represented a new paradigm in which attackers hijacked authorization systems and privileged personnel rather than stealing vault keys, according to security researchers and lawyers [6][11][13]. Hong Kong lawyer Joshua Chu explained that the attacker may not have stolen the vault key but hijacked the system that tells the vault to release money [6][11]. Check Point chief information security officer Jayant Dave said the Binance losses make it the largest crypto theft on record and that attackers now target systems and people with authority rather than vault keys [29]. NyxLab co-founder Kok Tin Gan said the breach appeared to involve a single signature granting the attacker full, non-expiring access to a Binance hot wallet [14]. Bernardo Brites, CEO of Trace Finance, warned that defending against hacks is becoming harder because AI can find vulnerabilities faster than they can be patched, predicting users would abandon smaller exchanges [8][24][31].

A coordinated industry response froze roughly $2.7 to $3 billion of the stolen assets within hours, with Tether freezing nearly $1.57 billion, Circle more than $800 million, Ripple about $200 million, and World Liberty Financial working with BitGo to freeze additional funds [8][12][27][31]. However, Patrick Tan, general counsel of ChainArgos, noted that no company issues or can freeze bitcoin or ether, meaning those funds can only be stopped if sent to a cooperating exchange or if law enforcement obtains private keys [6][11][13]. Elliptic's Robinson said the remaining $3.3 billion is mostly bitcoin, ether, and XRP that no one can freeze, and that some seizure may occur in coming months but the frozen share is unlikely to grow significantly [24]. Trace Finance's Brites said the longer time passes, the less likely attackers can fold funds into other exchanges or payment providers because the assets become blacklisted [6][11].

The hack exposed the structural fragility of centralized crypto finance, according to analysts and market participants [2][8][12]. Macquarie University's Sean Foley warned that assets held at a centralised exchange are only as safe as the exchange itself, and that the larger the exchange's holdings, the larger a target it becomes [2][24]. HashKey Group's Tim Sun said the incident could further dampen risk appetite and prompt investors to reassess the safety of assets held on centralised exchanges and risks around counterparties, solvency, and liquidity [8][12][24]. Standard Chartered's Jeffrey Kendrick said prices have further to fall until frozen assets are released and more details emerge, arguing the market has not reached a new support floor [9]. Bitcoin fell to a six-week low of around $76,368, ether dropped to a two-month low, and crypto-linked stocks including Coinbase and Robinhood declined [2][5][8].

Regulators across multiple jurisdictions responded to the hack, with some framing it as vindication of licensing and supervision regimes. US Commodity Futures Trading Commission chair Michael Selig said the hack proves the case for a proposed federal framework, arguing unregulated offshore exchanges lack US-style safeguards [34]. The UK Financial Conduct Authority said it was aware of the incident and engaging with Binance, though Binance was not registered to provide crypto services in the UK [12]. Thailand's Securities and Exchange Commission said no significant impact on Thai client assets had been found and instructed licensed operators to monitor risk and verify asset security [15]. The UAE Financial Services Regulatory Authority said it was engaging closely with Binance [36]. EU watchdogs ESMA and national regulators had already been examining Binance's use of the MiCA reverse-solicitation exemption before the hack, with ESMA stating the exemption should be treated as the exception and not used to circumvent requirements [48].

Binance's reimbursement pledge drew skepticism from lawyers and analysts. London-based lawyer Daniel Tunkel said customers' ability to recover losses depends on what their contracts promise and on where Binance can be sued for non-performance [5][43]. Abu Dhabi-based lawyer Kokila Alagh said the response will be critical and attention will turn to how commitments are delivered, the safe restoration of services, and the transparency of disclosures [36]. Some coverage drew parallels to Sam Bankman-Fried's pre-collapse assurances at FTX [5]. Binance's SAFU fund holds roughly $1 billion in bitcoin, more than six times less than the stolen amount, though the exchange said it would also use company capital [2][7].

The US Secret Service said it was working around the clock to identify, freeze, trace, and recover the stolen funds [5][43]. The FBI said it was aware of the incident and coordinating with public and private partners [34][38]. Binance said it was working with law enforcement to identify the criminals and bring them to justice [29][42]. The exchange aimed to resume withdrawals within 45 hours and publish a proof-of-reserves report [13][20][31].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
