COP31 Climate Talks End Without Agreement as Fossil Fuel Language Divides Nations

The EU and more than 130 countries rejected a draft omitting any mention of fossil fuels, while oil-producing states defended the text and the Turkish-Australian presidency said it exhausted every option.

COP31, the UN climate summit held in Antalya, Türkiye, ended without a final agreement after negotiations ran about two days past schedule, making it the longest UN climate conference on record [4][6][16]. The collapse marked the first time since COP15 in Copenhagen in 2009 that a summit closed without a cover decision [14][22][29][30]. COP31 President Murat Kurum, Türkiye's environment minister, told the closing plenary that there was no consensus on the proposed text and that no new draft would be issued [3][11][28].

The central dispute was whether the agreement should mention fossil fuels at all. The EU, the UK, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Norway, and Vanuatu rejected the draft — known as the Antalya Pact — because it contained no reference to fossil fuels or to transitioning away from them [4][6][9][17]. Irish Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien said it was "unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no mention of fossil fuels" [4][7]. German environment minister Carsten Schneider said some countries do not want the term fossil fuels used because they profit from them [9]. Spanish minister Sara Aagesen said science is clear that fossil fuels must be abandoned urgently [9][15]. The EU stated it would not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel [31]. Greenpeace Brasil said having no cover agreement was better than a bad one [1]. Former UN climate chief Christiana Figueres said the refusal of more than 130 countries to sign an inadequate deal should not be seen as failure but as "a sign of courage" [26].

Saudi Arabia and Russia opposed any explicit reference to fossil fuels in the text [5][9][26][27]. Saudi climate negotiator Mohammad Ayoub said "climate change requires all solutions on the table" and that Arab states account for only a small share of historical greenhouse gas emissions [4][6][22]. Russian delegate Sergei Kononuchenko said shrinking oil and gas supplies would lead to dirtier fuels and energy poverty and that the first to suffer would be the poorest [31]. Russia warned against turning to slogans over coal, oil, and gas [4]. The draft included language on carbon capture and storage, which Saudi Arabia defended as a legitimate climate solution [28][31].

Climate-vulnerable states argued the text offered false solutions that traded their survival for fossil-fuel interests. The Marshall Islands said it would not be forced to accept false solutions and would not compromise its future to benefit fossil fuel interests [7][31]. Vanuatu said the text "falls short of what international law requires, what science demands and what our vulnerable communities urgently need" [31]. Tuvalu's climate minister Maina Talia called the inclusion of a fossil fuel reference "a red line for us" [21]. Panama's environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro demanded an explicit commitment to exit fossil fuel production and accused what he called the petroleum mafia of holding the talks hostage [19]. Vanuatu's Ralph Regenvanu said carbon capture and storage had been singled out for special attention it does not deserve [7].

The Turkish-Australian presidency defended its handling of the negotiations. Kurum said some countries became fixated on a single word or expression [3] and said the text was not imposed on anyone [24]. Australian minister Chris Bowen, who served as COP31 President of Negotiations, said the talks faced "unbridgeable red lines" in "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory" [4][26]. Bowen said Australia left nothing on the field [11][26]. Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese attributed the failure to "difficult geopolitical times" [4][6].

Several developing-country delegations argued that an imperfect agreement preserved through consensus would have served multilateralism better than collapse. Pakistan said "we need to build bridges not barriers" and that "climate action is not a menu from which we can select only what suits us" [7][11][28]. Ghana said "Africa has always chosen to build multilateralism rather than walk away from it" [11]. India's environment minister Bhupender Yadav said India engaged constructively and tirelessly and remained committed to dialogue until the very end [14][45]. Nepal said "No es bueno volver a casa sin una decisión de cobertura" (It is not good to return home without a cover decision) [15].

African negotiators and civil-society campaigners argued the deeper failure was finance [18][22]. Power Shift Africa's Mohamed Adow said rich nations and polluting governments cannot keep arriving empty-handed and called COP31 the worst COP [4][23]. Adow accused the EU of hypocrisy, saying its governments demand fossil fuel roadmaps from others while expanding fossil fuels themselves [11][12]. Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign said "you can't ask developing countries to transform their economies while they're drowning in disasters and debt" [7]. A proposed $120 billion adaptation finance target was dropped from the draft [6][16][18]. EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said talks on new climate finance commitments were "dead in the water" [26].

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed that COP31 has ended without consensus" and that "there may be no consensus in the negotiating room" but "there is no ambiguity in the science" [11][28][31]. Guterres accused fossil-fuel interests of using "a page from Big Tobacco's playbook" [4][22]. Former US Vice President Al Gore called it "outrageous that a handful of petro-states have veto power over any mention of fossil fuels" and said the COP process needs significant reform [4][6][32]. Panama demanded changes to consensus rules so disputes could be decided by vote [9]. Global Justice Now director Nick Dearden said "this must be the last time we allow fossil fuel interests, which are profiting so handsomely from the climate crisis, to hold us to ransom" [21]. Observatório do Clima's Claudio Angelo criticized the dual presidency as weak and split between two countries with little tradition in climate diplomacy [21].

UN officials and analysts argued the climate process survived the collapse. UN climate chief Simon Stiell said the consensus process "has worked to get us where we are" but "certainly fell short today" [4][6][11]. Several decisions were adopted on oceans and other agenda items [13][41]. A "35 by 35" electrification pledge — raising electricity's share of final energy demand to 35% by 2035 — drew more than 75 countries and 180 businesses [24][26]. About 20 countries presented fossil fuel exit roadmaps outside the formal text [23]. IDDRI's Céline Kauffmann said countries "Les pays n'étaient pas prêts à faire un pas l'un vers l'autre" (were not ready to make a step toward each other) but that advances, coalitions and initiatives were launched and will continue [23]. Ethiopia's foreign minister Gedion Timothewos, the COP32 president-designate, said Ethiopia aims to "restore hope, rebuild trust and mobilize collective action" at the next summit in Addis Ababa [7][28].

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
