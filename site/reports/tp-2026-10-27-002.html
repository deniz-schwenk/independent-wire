Student-Led Movement Defeats Vucic in Serbian Parliamentary Election

Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat as the Student List secured a projected majority on record turnout, with the outcome described as both democratic restoration and foreign-engineered change.

Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat in Serbia's snap parliamentary election on October 25, 2026, telling supporters at SNS headquarters that "based on a stratified sample, it is clear that we have lost these elections" [3][8][15][35]. The Student List, a movement that grew out of protests triggered by the November 2024 Novi Sad railway canopy collapse that killed 16 people, was projected to win 135 to 140 of 250 parliamentary seats with approximately 53 percent of the vote, while Vucic's SNS-led coalition was projected to take about 105 seats with 39 percent; the official count from 96 percent of polling stations showed 140 seats to 103 [2][8][27]. Turnout reached 68.8 percent on the official count, with preliminary estimates ranging from 67.6 to nearly 70 percent — the highest level since Yugoslavia's breakup in 1992 [8][13][16][27].

The Student List's lead candidate, cardiologist Ilija Srdanović, said the country is "moving toward a more-civilized and better Serbia" and that "this will be a free state" [2][4]. Student List candidate Radomir Saicic told supporters that "no force can forever overpower a people who have decided to take their destiny into their own hands" [2]. University of Belgrade political scientist Nebojša Vladisavljević described the result as "an avalanche that has wiped out an authoritarian regime and entire political scene," giving the movement "absolute legitimacy to govern" [2]. Celebrating residents gathered in Belgrade and Novi Sad, with one telling the BBC, "We are all crying... We couldn't believe that it would be so easy" [2], and another saying, "We can finally breathe again" [8].

Vucic attributed the defeat to foreign interference, calling the result "a verification of a revolution carried out with support from various European countries and the American deep state" opposed to Donald Trump [4][26][29]. He accused Serbia's independent media and unnamed European countries of engineering the outcome [4]. SNS Presidency member Aleksandra Tomić described the result as a two-year externally engineered project and warned of economic risks, stating that one lost battle does not mean a lost war [36]. Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico, who campaigned alongside Vucic in Novi Sad, urged voters to be leaders who could say no to external demands [20].

European leaders welcomed the result as an opening for Serbia's EU accession path. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the EU stands ready to support reforms on the rule of law, media freedom, anti-corruption, and democratic institutions [4][31]. Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos stated that "a new chapter can begin for Serbia" and that "the door to the EU is open" [2][33]. Hungarian Prime Minister Péter Magyar, the first foreign leader to call and congratulate the Student List, said his own April victory over Viktor Orbán had given the movement strength [2][33][44]. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky called the result "a new chapter" and thanked Serbia for respecting Ukraine's independence [12][44], while a US State Department spokesperson said the United States looks forward to expanding its partnership with Serbia [32].

The Kremlin acknowledged the result while signaling its expectation of continued partnership. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov praised Vucic's contribution to bilateral relations and said Moscow hopes the new authorities will understand that "cooperation with Russia is in Serbia's interests," adding that "it takes two to tango" [2][30][39]. Student List representative Jovo Bakic stated that the movement respects Russia as a great power, historical partner, and protector, and that there will be no changes in that orientation [26]. The Student List has pledged not to impose sanctions on Russia, to maintain Serbia's military neutrality, and not to seek NATO membership [20][26][30].

Analysts cautioned that converting a protest movement into a functioning government presents substantial challenges. University of Graz professor Florian Bieber said Vucic conceded because even with all means of election manipulation he could no longer falsify the result, but that a broad movement can topple a government without being able to govern [31]. Austrian Institute for International Politics researcher Vedran Dzihic said that toppling the regime is one thing and establishing a new democratic beginning is another [31]. Political analyst Dusan Vucicevic noted that the movement needed both pro-European and Eurosceptic voters to win, and that no prime ministerial candidate has been named [5]. Centre for Contemporary Politics programme director Nikola Burazer said the process of choosing top officials will be complex and cannot be decided by a small group [25].

Marko Todorovic of the Center for European Policy said he expects a significant change in Serbia-EU relations but no sudden break with Russia or China and no imminent sanctions on Moscow [28]. Carnegie Europe senior fellow Dimitar Bechev said strong nationalist voices in the Student List want to keep Russia onside because of Kosovo [33]. An unnamed EU diplomat said the Student List's foreign policy is unclear and that many EU countries will be watching closely [33]. University of Belgrade professor Bojan Vranić cautioned against excessive EU enthusiasm, saying the Student List has been very conservative toward the bloc [33].

Domestic and international monitors documented systemic irregularities during the campaign and on election day. CRTA reported ballot photographing in every fourth polling station by noon, with public-sector employees pressured to prove SNS votes by photograph [18]. CeSID recorded more than 300 possible violations by 20:00, including out-of-polling-station voting and family voting [17]. ODIHR mission head Eoghan Murphy warned that unregulated online campaigning harms transparency and that state media failed to provide impartial information [25]. Special Coordinator Gilles Baum said the election unfolded amid profound polarisation with "the equality of campaign opportunity seriously compromised," though CRTA's Raša Nedeljkov said record numbers of domestic observers largely prevented systemic manipulation at polling stations [25].

Kosovar officials focused on Serbian campaign rhetoric rather than congratulating the winners. Kosovo's Assembly Speaker Albulena Haxhiu noted that Milan Radoičić, who publicly claimed responsibility for an attack on Kosovo, appeared freely at an SNS rally [40]. Deputy Prime Minister Glauk Konjufca said Serbian officials' territorial claims demonstrate that Belgrade's ambitions persist [40]. Bosnian Foreign Minister Elmedin Konaković praised the students' courage but said future relations require full respect for Bosnia's sovereignty and territorial integrity [41]. SNSD leader Milorad Dodik congratulated the Student List and said Republika Srpska and Serbia move forward together [41][44].

Vucic stated he does not want unrest or violence on the streets and pledged responsible acceptance of defeat [18][19]. Former Serbian President Boris Tadic, who said he waited 14 years for the ouster, warned against score-settling and urged restoring order [20]. Serbian Orthodox Patriarch Porfirije urged voters to vote by conscience and accept the outcome without hatred [38]. Council of Europe Secretary-General Alain Berset welcomed Vucic's acceptance of the result and commitment to a peaceful democratic transition [4][44].

The Student List faces constitutional deadlines to form a government, with the acting presidency capped at three months and a presidential election expected in December [37][14]. Constitutional Court President Vladan Petrov explained that "until a new parliament comes to life, the previous one does not die; until a government is elected, the existing one remains in a technical mandate" [37]. Ana Brnabić simultaneously serves as assembly speaker and acting president until a new president is elected [37][45]. Dejan Šoškić has been mentioned as a possible prime ministerial candidate [25].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia

affected_community

legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.

Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.

Armed forces personnel, commanders, and defense ministries.
