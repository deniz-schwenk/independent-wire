Colombia Earthquake Death Toll Rises Past 240 as Rescue Operations Continue and Aid Disputes Intensify

The government declares an economic emergency and vows reconstruction, while communities in Chocó report being cut off and critics question the initial refusal of foreign rescue teams.

The death toll from the August 10 earthquake in western Colombia has risen to at least 239–241, with thousands reported missing and rescue operations stretching into their third day [1][2][3][10]. The government later updated the official count to 265 dead and 496 missing [6][15]. The National Unit for Disaster Risk Management reported 239 deaths, 3,755 injured, and 287 missing, with over 9,000 homes destroyed across 14 departments and 403 municipalities [3]. Civilian-run databases suggest the number of unaccounted-for people could approach 4,000, far exceeding official figures [7][23].

President Abelardo de la Espriella, who took office three days before the quake, declared a national state of emergency and announced the creation of a reconstruction fund, stating: "This is undoubtedly a tragedy of immense proportions. Our efforts are focused on those who remain missing" [6][13][34]. De la Espriella later invoked an "economic emergency" under the constitution to create what he called a "miracle fund" for rebuilding hospitals, schools, homes, and infrastructure, and announced rent subsidies for families whose homes were damaged [15][18]. Cali Mayor Alejandro Eder reported 95 deaths, 949 injured, and 239 people trapped under rubble in the city, while mayors in Manizales and Calima reported collapsed buildings and deaths [10][4][13]. The president announced the mobilization of the entire military and police apparatus, including engineers, rescuers, and search dogs, to the affected regions [10].

In the Chocó department, near the epicenter of San José del Palmar, local officials and community leaders described a response that had not reached them. León Fabio Marín Moncada, mayor of San José del Palmar, said: "Nos hemos sentido abandonados, y no solamente en este momento. Es duro decirlo, pero en el transcurrir de la vida el Chocó se ha sentido abandonado" (We have felt abandoned, and not only at this moment. It is hard to say, but throughout life, Chocó has felt abandoned) [49]. Marín reported blocked roads, no potable water, intermittent communication, and food shortages [49]. The Ombudsman's office warned that some indigenous communities in Chocó remained unreachable two days after the quake [39]. In Quibdó, residents described families sleeping outdoors and social organizations setting up community kitchens in the absence of government aid [42]. Samir Valencia, a former resident of a collapsed building in Cali, said: "We've always been forgotten in Buenaventura. All the help is centred around the city of Manizales, in Armenia and Bogota, which were hardly affected at all" [34].

The government's handling of international assistance generated competing accounts. Multiple sources reported that Colombia initially limited its requests to supplies rather than personnel, declining offers of foreign search-and-rescue teams [25][26][44][46]. El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele stated he was told by Colombian authorities that personnel support was not required, only supplies, and dispatched 51 tons of humanitarian aid [11][47]. Foreign Minister Omar Bula rejected reports that the government turned down international aid, stating his office was coordinating shipments from Peru, Mexico, and El Salvador [6]. Opposition congresswoman Maria Fernanda Carrascal said the decision was ideological, stating the government only wanted rescue workers from Israel and the US while Mexican teams had been ready since Monday [47]. Civil engineer Gina Villalobos questioned the technical rationale for rejecting specialized international assistance given the number of affected areas [47]. Chinese ambassador Zhu Jingyang called on the government to release the name of the official coordinating international aid [47].

International aid pledges materialized as the survival window narrowed. The United States pledged $15.5 million in emergency aid and deployed urban search-and-rescue teams from Fairfax County and Los Angeles County [13][20][23]. The European Union released 2 million euros, announced by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen [32]. Ecuador and Venezuela offered rescue teams [18]. The UN redirected pre-positioned supplies and deployed staff, with UN Coordinator for Colombia María Torres stating priorities included shelter, health, food security, and protection, with particular attention to women and children [20][21]. WHO reported damage to 24 health facilities, including the partial collapse of Hospital Universitario del Valle's cardiology and pediatric tower in Cali [21].

Civil society organizations and volunteers mobilized independently to fill gaps in the official response. In Medellín, the Saciar foundation's food bank collected 35 tonnes of donations in 48 hours [32]. Volunteer rescuer Cristian Nieves in Cali said: "We haven't stopped. We've been doing this all night" [34]. In Pereira, over 500 volunteers spontaneously joined rescue efforts [26]. Local volunteer Maximiliano Herrera criticized the government response as disorganized, stating: "It's not a lack of support. It's a lack of organisation. Because it's unbelievable that such a large city has no control over its agencies" [34].

Structural engineering experts attributed the scale of building collapses to widespread informal construction. Rafael Camilo Gutiérrez Melgarejo, a professor at Florida International University, stated: "En Colombia lastimosamente se tiene un índice muy alto de informalidad en la construcción, que está alrededor de 60 % y que realmente es muy alto" (Colombia unfortunately has a very high rate of informal construction, around 60%, which is really very high) [40]. Civil engineer Albert Ortiz explained that while the deep quake was predictable, the high percentage of informal construction in smaller municipalities led to greater structural failures [40]. Scientists attributed the earthquake's severity to Nazca-plate subduction dynamics amplified by local geology, with geologist Raul Perez describing a "site effect" where geological formations increased seismic wave strength and duration [7][8]. Colombian Geological Survey director Julio Fierro Morales confirmed over 130 aftershocks and warned against misinformation, stating: "La ciencia aún no puede predecir los sismos" (Science still cannot predict earthquakes) [29].

Analysts framed the disaster as a political test for de la Espriella, who ran on a platform of cutting public spending. Tiziano Breda, a senior analyst at ACLED, noted: "De La Espriella ran on a platform to cut public finances, including defunding the state's risk management unit. This unit is currently at the forefront of the emergency response" [6]. German outlet Der Spiegel highlighted the president's lack of administrative experience and the risk that failure to manage the crisis would fall solely on him [9]. Human rights researcher Juana Corral warned that armed groups in Chocó would exploit the earthquake's chaos to increase forced recruitment and blockades, linking the disaster to the collapse of the peace process under the new administration [50].

Survivors and bereaved families described personal loss amid the ongoing rescue efforts. Santiago Lloreda, who lost his mother and 13-month-old daughter, said: "My daughter was a dream made reality. My mother was the pillar of our family" [6]. In Pereira, Marcela Perez waited for news of her partner's 11-year-old daughter trapped in a collapsed building, stating: "We are just waiting and trusting in God" [30]. Bogotá fire official Camilo Cano stated that rescue teams still had hope of finding survivors up to four days after the quake [30].

Rescue operations are entering what Cali Mayor Alejandro Eder called "the final phase," with the 72-hour survival window closing [45]. The full death toll may take days to establish as communication blackouts persist in rural Chocó and aftershocks continue [16][29]. The USGS has estimated reconstruction costs between $1 billion and $10 billion [26].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society
Non-state organizations representing collective interests (NGOs, human rights groups, trade unions, religious bodies).
government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.
industry
