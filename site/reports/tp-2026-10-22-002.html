US Orders Diplomats to Leave Saudi Arabia as Houthi Strikes Continue

State Department says departure covers Saudi Arabia only; UK deploys fighter jets as France, Egypt, and Turkey announce defensive support.

The United States ordered nonemergency government employees and their family members to leave Saudi Arabia on October 21, citing Houthi attacks on civilian airports and infrastructure [3][5][22]. State Department spokesperson Tommy Pigott said the ordered departure applied to Saudi Arabia only and was driven by Houthi hostilities, including strikes on civilian airports and critical infrastructure causing casualties and severe travel disruption [3][10][22]. The move came as the United Kingdom announced the deployment of RAF Typhoon fighter jets to help defend Saudi cities and infrastructure, and as Houthi missile and drone strikes continued to target airports and oil facilities across the kingdom [12][17][18].

The scope of the drawdown is contested. Pigott explicitly denied reports of wider Gulf evacuations, calling them "inaccurate" and stating that the ordered departure covered "non-emergency U.S. government employees and their family members in Saudi Arabia only" [7][10]. However, Reuters, citing three US officials, and AFP, citing a diplomatic source, reported that the State Department was preparing ordered departures across Gulf missions, with all staff and families ordered out of Saudi Arabia and families told to leave the UAE, Jordan, Qatar, and Bahrain [19][21]. Aaj Tak reported that the embassy in Riyadh issued a security alert warning of further Houthi attacks and that the scope of any evacuation would depend on conditions [2]. ANSA, citing a diplomatic source, linked the order to the possibility of renewed hostilities with Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthi rebels [1]. CNN Arabic reported that other US missions in the region were also adjusting staffing [24].

The Houthi attacks that prompted the drawdown have killed and wounded civilians across Saudi Arabia. A strike on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam killed one person and injured eight, according to the Saudi Civil Aviation Authority [5][8]. The Saudi Energy Ministry reported that debris from an intercepted drone targeting the Shaybah oil field killed two foreign workers — a Filipino and a Nepali national — and injured two others [5][6][7]. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki stated that Houthi attacks on Riyadh and Abha airports this month killed 19 people and injured more than 300 [16]. UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting condemned the attacks as "absolutely despicable" [16][17][18]. A Riyadh resident, engineering consultant Jaber Al Siwat, told RFI that "La vie continue, les gens ne paniquent pas" (Life continues, people don't panic), crediting missile defense systems and blaming Iran for supplying weapons to the Houthis [26].

A coalition of European and regional partners has announced military support for Saudi Arabia, each insisting its role is strictly limited. The UK Ministry of Defence announced the deployment of RAF Typhoon Eurofighters, an A400M transport aircraft, and military advisers for defensive missions to protect Saudi cities, ports, airports, and oil infrastructure [12][17][18]. The deployment builds on a UK-Saudi military relationship stretching from the 1915 Treaty of Darin through the 1985 Al-Yamamah oil-for-arms deal to the 72-Typhoon purchase in 2007 [20]. French President Emmanuel Macron made a surprise visit to Riyadh, committing troops and air defense systems to protect the Red Sea port city of Yanbu and agreeing to extend diesel deliveries and expand energy-infrastructure cooperation [7][16]. Egyptian President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi said Egypt's military support "no incluye el lanzamiento de ataques contra Yemen" (does not include launching attacks against Yemen) and was limited to defending Saudi territory [9]. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan stated that "Türkiye başka bir ülke toprağına asker gönderip orada bir saldırı yapmayacak" (Turkey will not send troops to another country's territory to carry out attacks there) [33].

Security analysts question whether these deployments will alter the conflict's trajectory or whether the defensive label holds under scrutiny. RUSI analyst HA Hellyer asked where defensive ends and offensive begins, warning that "the UK might say it was defensive but the Houthis would see it as offensive" [18]. RUSI research fellow Urban Coningham said UK jets would conduct defensive patrols shooting down incoming missiles while also refuelling Saudi aircraft heading into Yemeni airspace — a role the Houthis may read as offensive participation [16]. ECFR policy fellow Camille Lons said France's assistance was "a way to show support [for Saudi Arabia] in times of crisis, and a way to deepen the defence partnership" [16]. While UK coverage has presented the British deployment as the most significant military mission signed off by Prime Minister Andy Burnham, defense researcher Akram Kharief described it as likely limited to 12–24 aircraft and "more symbolic than a game changer" [12][18]. King's College senior fellow Nawaf Obaid argued the British Typhoons could "reinforce Saudi air defences and, crucially, free up Saudi Eurofighters for offensive operations" [16].

Washington has repeatedly declined Saudi requests to participate directly in strikes against the Houthis. President Donald Trump rejected multiple Saudi requests in September, offering only intelligence and targeting support, and stated he does not intend to restart the war before the November 3 elections [8][16][30]. Trump later said the United States "may" join Saudi strikes on Houthi positions but that no final decision had been made [13][24]. US envoy Tom Barrack told a conference in Qatar that sending ground troops against the Houthis "seems fruitless" [18]. Former State Department adviser Aaron David Miller said the administration avoided a wider clash with Iran and a new front in Yemen, partly due to the inconclusive 52-day Operation Rough Rider campaign and domestic politics before midterms [34].

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree denied that Houthi forces carried out "deep" operations in Saudi territory while claiming a ballistic missile strike on a Saudi operations room in Badr camp in Aden [6]. Houthi political bureau member Mohammed al-Bukhaiti warned that any US intervention would meet a swift and painful response targeting US interests in the region and called on Saudi oil and airport workers to stay away from those sites [25]. Saree declared Saudi airspace the center of the group's military operations and issued a warning to international airlines against using it [27]. On the Taiz front, Houthi forces used drone swarms, artillery, Katyusha rockets, and ballistic missiles, tightening the siege of Yemen's third-largest city and cutting supply routes [15]. Yemeni scholar Fatima Asrar said the Houthi offensive was entirely expected and that the group's manpower is among its most underestimated capabilities [31].

Iran denies arming the Houthis and warns that outside interference will complicate the conflict. Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi said Iran is fully prepared to defend itself and warned that any interference by Egypt or other countries would make the situation more difficult [4][6]. President Masoud Pezeshkian said Washington cannot expect dialogue while it is blockading and threatening Iran [4]. Iran also rejected allegations by Saudi Arabia and the United States that it supports the Houthis [17].

Saudi Arabia is broadening its security partnerships beyond the United States. Analysts frame Riyadh's new defence pact with Turkey and Pakistan — the Mecca Pact, which has not yet been ratified — and its diversification of partnerships since 2015 as a deliberate strategy to broaden security options [16][34]. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly rejected Israeli military assistance despite US and Israeli urging, because cooperation with Israel would play badly with Saudi public opinion [35]. Former Saudi foreign ministry adviser Salem Al-Yami rejected the idea of US disengagement, saying Washington cannot afford distance from a key ally [34]. Carnegie Endowment director Amr Hamzawy said withholding direct military aid does not prove the US is withdrawing from the region, describing a shift to intelligence cooperation [34].

The conflict is transmitting directly into energy and mobility costs. Oil prices rose after the drawdown order, with LSEG analysts warning that Iranian targeting of tankers beyond the Strait of Hormuz jeopardizes Gulf crude loadings [4][23]. Saudi Arabia postponed its flagship Future Investment Initiative conference until 2027 after banks raised security concerns [17]. The closure of Riyadh's airport drove private jet charter prices up more than 50 percent, with war-risk insurance adding up to $20,000 per large jet flight [11]. The Trump administration offered to loan up to nearly a third of the 1-million-barrel US emergency heating oil reserve to contain diesel prices [4].

Inside Yemen, the United Nations documented a tightening Houthi siege of Taiz, severe shortages of food, fuel, and medicine, and cholera and measles outbreaks, with health funding under 14 percent covered [36]. UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric urged all parties to uphold international humanitarian law and refrain from attacks on civilians [36]. WHO official Annette Heinzelmann warned that "without urgent funding and unimpeded access to safe water, vaccination and essential health services, outbreaks could spread rapidly among already vulnerable communities" [36]. Migrant-sending governments counted their dead and wounded workers: the Philippines reported seven Filipinos injured, three in critical condition, and called for a cessation of hostilities [28][29]. Nepal detailed casualties by nationality at Riyadh airport and cancelled Himalaya Airlines flights [27].

Russian and Chinese-linked commentary addresses the crisis in the context of the US-led Gulf security architecture. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said Russia was watching the conflict with concern, calling the situation "крайне тревожная" (extremely alarming), and noted no party had requested Russian mediation [32]. Chinese academic commentary analyzed the escalation dynamics and US electoral constraints, while Al-Monitor argued the Houthi advance near Bab al-Mandeb exposes a gap in the US-led Gulf security system and described China's hedging between protecting its shipping access and preserving Saudi stability [30][31].

The State Department's ordered departure remains in effect for Saudi Arabia, with the FAA urging US airlines to exercise caution over Saudi airspace and the US Embassy requiring government employees to obtain special permission to use any airport [13][14]. The UK Typhoon deployment and French air defense commitment to Yanbu have been announced [12][16]. Egypt's parliament has postponed a vote authorizing military support for Riyadh [9]. Trump has said the US "may" join Saudi strikes but no decision has been announced [13][24].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
