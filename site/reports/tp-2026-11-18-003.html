Nvidia Board Rules Out Jensen Huang's Return as Founder Signals Willingness to Resume CEO Role

Huang admits "serious mistakes" and says he would return if asked; board chair Harvey Jones says the board "does not intend to reinstate" him, as congressional probes and tightened export controls proceed.

Nvidia's board formally ruled out reinstating co-founder Jensen Huang as CEO on November 17, with board chairman Harvey Jones telling an earnings call that "the Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO" [6][22][25]. The announcement came hours after Huang issued his first public statement since resigning, saying he would be "honored" to return if the board judged it "in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America" [2][5][13]. Huang acknowledged "serious mistakes" in his personal life and said, "I created problems for the Board that I deeply regret. I own my mistakes completely" [5][25]. His resignation followed reports of a relationship with a woman suspected of Chinese espionage and questions about whether restricted Nvidia chips reached China through diversion channels [13][21]. Jones said the board is "proactively dealing with the consequences" of what he called "an unexpected transition" and has launched a global CEO search [30][25]. The board has hired Heidrick & Struggles to find a permanent successor and is considering both internal and external candidates [12]. Nvidia also created a board-level National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee chaired by Suzanne Nora Johnson, a move President Donald Trump welcomed as "a good idea" [25][26][22]. Jones said Nvidia is "committed to working constructively with government and industry partners to help establish and advance a robust framework" to safeguard national security [26].

Interim CEO Colette Kress, presenting Nvidia's first quarterly results since Huang's departure, said it is "business as usual" and "our momentum is real" [7][24][25]. Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion, more than double the previous year, and forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $130 billion [9][12]. Kress said Nvidia's partners and suppliers are "100% behind us" and that the product roadmap, including the Vera Rubin chip, remains on track [24][25]. Will Rhind, CEO of GraniteShares Advisors, said the earnings call without Huang "was a signal for the bulls that life carries on without him and the foundation the company has built is as robust as ever" [22]. Analyst Anshel Sag of Moor Insights & Strategy said near-term operations will not be affected because the executive leadership team "is capable and has followed his leadership and executed effectively for years" [12].

Other analysts warned that Nvidia's success beyond Huang is less certain. Ray Wang, CEO of Constellation Research, said Nvidia can perform for "two to three years" without Huang but that sustaining success beyond that could be challenging because "Jensen created the market, Jensen created the trust, and Jensen is that ecosystem" [6][25]. Nvidia executive Nader Khalil said the company "cannot make an improvised leadership transition" and will not fare well in decisive years without Huang [15]. Investor Rick Meckler of Cherry Lane Investments said the leadership story is "very complicated" and "hard not to factor that in as an additional risk for people buying stock" [12]. Senior analyst Daniela Hathorn of Capital.com said the results temper worries that Huang was irreplaceable, but "the harder question is whether Nvidia can preserve the strategic advantage Huang helped build" [24].

Technology figures, investors, and employees have called for Huang's reinstatement or at minimum a fair hearing before any final judgment. An online petition gathered more than 180,000 signatures — counts approaching 200,000 were reported as the campaign grew — including Elon Musk, Tim Sweeney, Garry Tan, and over 7,500 Nvidia employees, with Uber co-founder Travis Kalanick among its organizers [3][4][8][22][27][28]. Garry Tan said "Jensen deserves due process, not whatever is happening right now" [32]. Kalanick demanded "a fair process and independent investigation" before any final decision, arguing suspicion alone should not end Huang's leadership [27]. Investor Michael Monaghan of Founder ETFs said Nvidia's earnings come "from the machine that Jensen built" and that iconic founders are hard to replace [24]. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives described the board's position as caught "between a founder who has publicly asked for his job back, a shareholder base with the means to force the question, and an administration split between a president open to his return and a Treasury secretary demanding an outsider" [2]. Ives separately stated there is no chance Huang returns as CEO [32], and Anthony Scaramucci, founder of SkyBridge, said the prospect is "not happening" [32].

President Trump said he "would not mind" Huang returning and that he believed Huang "learned a big lesson," while saying he was not sure the woman linked to him is a Chinese agent [11][20][30]. Trump held up to $62 million in Nvidia stock, and Nvidia shares had fallen 16 percent since November 8 [28]. Trump told Russia's Kommersant he "may discuss" the alleged Chinese espionage against Nvidia with Xi Jinping at the APEC summit [45], while senior U.S. officials told CNBC the Nvidia situation "should not really have any bearing on the trip" and that the U.S. does not expect to brief China on ongoing national-security investigations [36].

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Nvidia "must look outside the company for its next leader" and needs "radical change" [3][20][23]. Bessent questioned whether Nvidia had a "culture of noncompliance" on chip export restrictions and asked whether the company was "pressing these seemingly illicit sales to goose their revenues and their earnings" [32][34]. Senator Maria Cantwell said the U.S. "cannot maintain AI leadership if the responsible agency does not enforce its own controls" [11]. A Yale School of Management commentary by Jeffrey Sonnenfeld and Bill George argued the board should rule out Huang's return because of workplace affairs with subordinates and unresolved questions about chip flows to China, citing securities-fraud and governance risk [34].

Republican committee chairmen Brian Mast, John Moolenaar, and Rick Crawford said "the American people need answers on Mr. Huang and Nvidia's role in allowing dangerous PRC actors to access our most advanced chips, as well as the role of a potential Chinese intelligence asset in this scandal" [2]. Huang agreed to transcribed interviews before several House committees on December 1, and Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter will appear on December 2 [10][15]. Huang also agreed to testify publicly before the Senate Banking Committee, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick reportedly agreed to testify as well [38][14]. The U.S. Commerce and Justice Departments have investigated Singapore-based cloud computing firm Megaspeed since 2024, and Nvidia placed Megaspeed on an internal no-sell list in June 2025 after a Commerce Department request [13][22]. Former OpenAI researcher Will Deputy found that six Chinese AI models, including DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, and MiniMax-M3, carry fingerprints of export-restricted Nvidia Blackwell chips, based on analysis of 24 models and six trillion numerical combinations [21]. AI policy analyst Dean Ball compared the discovery to "finding a criminal's DNA at a crime scene" [21].

Beijing rejected the espionage allegations, with Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun calling the so-called Chinese spy narrative "entirely groundless, speculative and intended to smear and malign China" [22][37]. Beijing also denied that the woman identified as Alice Huang is a Chinese intelligence asset [20][21]. Megaspeed denied wrongdoing and said media reports about its operations are "not supported by verifiable evidence" [22].

The U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security designated Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil as destinations with an "unacceptable risk of diversion" and required export licenses for advanced chips to those countries [22][27][43]. Brazil's Ministry of Development said it is monitoring the measure and cannot yet assess concrete effects [42]. Singapore's government said it is "carefully studying the new measure" and discussing its potential impact with companies [43]. Indonesian officials said they are reviewing the policy and seeking industry input [44]. Bart Groothuis, a Dutch Member of the European Parliament and negotiator of the EU chip plan, said Europe has "no other choice" because chip design is "almost entirely American" [41]. Japan's Deputy Chief Cabinet Secretary Masanao Ozaki said Japan will watch Nvidia developments, calling the company important for AI semiconductor supply [40].

Huang is scheduled to testify before House committees on December 1, with Teter appearing December 2 and a Senate Banking Committee hearing to follow [10][38]. The board's independent investigation, led by Joon H. Kim, continues alongside congressional and SEC probes [20][30].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
