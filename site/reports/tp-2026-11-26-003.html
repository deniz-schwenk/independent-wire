US Proposes Section 301 Tariffs on 14 Trading Partners, Citing Industrial Overcapacity

China faces duties of up to 40 percent as the EU, India, and Bangladesh also face new levies; partners with negotiated caps seek quiet resolution while Brazil contests the tariffs at the WTO.

The US Trade Representative proposed Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners on November 25, 2026, following an eight-month investigation into alleged structural excess industrial capacity [2][6][8]. The proposal sets a 20 percent additional levy on Chinese goods — rising to 40 percent if the US-China trade truce collapses — with the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Switzerland capped at 15 percent, India at 17 to 25 percent, Vietnam at 20 to 35 percent, Bangladesh at 18 to 20 percent, and Singapore at up to 20 percent [1][5][6][13][26]. US officials indicated, however, that some rates could rise by a few percentage points, with the EU, Japan, South Korea, and Switzerland potentially facing 20 percent [6][9][13]. Mexico and Norway were excluded from the proposal pending ongoing investigations [6][8][34]. Steel, aluminium, autos, energy, some semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, and food are expected to be exempt [8][24]. The measures would not take effect before public hearings scheduled for January 25, 2027 [26][29].

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer defended the duties as a response to decades of state-backed industrial strategies by trading partners. "For decades, many of our trading partners have adopted industrial strategies built on targeting manufacturing, wage and labor suppression, financial and other support, non-market economy inputs, and other market distorting practices that tilt the trade balance in their favor while eroding America's manufacturing base," Greer stated [25][28]. Greer said the proposed rates respect caps negotiated in existing trade agreements and that the tariffs would "level the playing field for American workers" [5][28]. Trade lawyer Ryan Majerus described the excess-capacity probe as among the most important Trump administration actions, warning that subsidized factories abroad risk oversupplying markets and hollowing out US manufacturing [7].

China's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun rejected the overcapacity premise, calling it "a false issue" and opposing its use "as an excuse for political manipulation" [5][27]. China's embassy in Washington said "the so-called issue of 'industrial overcapacity' does not really exist and should not be used as a pretext for political manipulation" [12]. The European Commission rejected the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity, with Deputy Chief Spokesperson Olof Gill stating that "Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity" and regretting the absence of joint US-EU action [18][27]. The EU insisted Washington honor the 15 percent tariff ceiling agreed with Commission President Ursula von der Leyen in July 2025 [8][22]. India denied any structural excess capacity and said the issue should be resolved through trade negotiations [1][12]. Switzerland also rejected the allegations from the US investigations [27].

The proposal follows a Supreme Court ruling that struck down Trump's April 2025 "Liberation Day" tariffs, which had been imposed under emergency economic powers [4][6][9]. Georgetown law professor Gregory Shaffer called the administration's use of Section 301 "cynical," saying the investigations were launched "to support the president's aim to set tariffs globally and to modify them at any time at the president's will, and thus strip Congress of the authority to set tariff rates under the taxing power set forth in the Constitution" [27]. Democratic Senator Ron Wyden vowed to "use every tool at my disposal to end Trump's tariffs chaos," calling the proposal another attempt to reconstruct illegal global tariffs [12]. Trade policy expert Dmitry Grozoubinski criticized the use of Section 301, saying "the legislation is supposed to be a scalpel, and they used it like a flamethrower" [2].

The tariff announcement coincided with US inflation data at 3.7 percent, nearly twice the Federal Reserve's target [6][36]. Portfolio manager Bradford Smith of Janus Henderson Investors said additional tariffs "will make the Fed's job more difficult" with inflation already an issue [36]. Rachel Ziemba, adjunct senior fellow at the Center for a New American Security, warned that the timing before the G20 "increases risk of both inflationary pressures and macro volatility in 2027" [27]. US stocks and gold fell following the announcement, with Asian stock markets also selling off sharply [27][37]. ING analysts said "while the legal justification for tariffs keeps changing, US protectionism is becoming increasingly difficult to unwind" [5][24].

Independent political analyst Hisham Wahby called the move hard to justify economically after Republican midterm losses, saying "this is not a free market economy when you're putting tariffs on everybody" and predicting small businesses would be hit hardest [2]. Dean Baker, senior economist at the Centre for Economic and Policy Research, said a coordinated US-EU-Canada front with clear ground rules for China would be more productive, describing Trump as "lashing out really indiscriminately" [11]. Derek Scissors of the American Enterprise Institute said the proposal "barely raises existing tariffs on China and will make no difference to the country's trade behaviour" [12].

Governments holding negotiated tariff ceilings treated the proposal as preliminary and manageable. South Korea's trade minister called the 15 percent rate "more than a decent outcome" because it falls within the previously agreed limit [28]. Taiwan's Executive Yuan spokeswoman Michelle Lee said Taipei is in close contact with Washington and that recommended rates are not final decisions [15]. Thai Commerce Ministry Deputy Director-General Khwanonpha Phiwnin urged calm, saying the proposal has not affected Thai exports and expressing confidence that the US-Thailand joint statement will keep Thailand's final rate within the previous 19 percent level [14]. Norway's Foreign Minister Espen Barth Eide welcomed Norway's exclusion, saying "Norwegian companies produce in response to market demand" [27]. Capital Economics chief North America economist Stephen Brown called the announcement "not as alarming as it looks" because it largely replaces previously announced summer tariffs [28]. Italy's Palazzo Chigi expressed cautious optimism and recommended avoiding attacks on the White House [47].

Bangladesh and Vietnam, whose economies depend heavily on exports, face rates that local industry groups and officials warn will erode competitiveness. Bangladesh already faces a 25 percent average US tariff after a 10 percent forced-labor levy imposed in July 2026, and the new proposal would add further pressure [19]. Mustafizur Rahman, honorary fellow at the Centre for Policy Dialogue, warned the tariffs could jeopardize Bangladesh's competitiveness and that US importers may push costs onto Bangladeshi exporters [19]. Bangladesh Cement Manufacturers Association president Ameerul Haq said additional tariffs would increase pressure on the cement industry and could cause bilateral misunderstanding [19]. Vietnam's Deputy Prime Minister Bui Thanh Son said the tariff rate is "not consistent" with the reality of US-Vietnam bilateral economic cooperation [28]. Vietnam's trade ministry said it is preparing rebuttal comments on the preliminary findings [12]. Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara said it is "regrettable that a proposed measure targeting Japan has been announced" [12][42].

Brazil rejected the measures as unacceptable coercion. President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva's office said "Brazil will not yield to such threats" [28]. Brazil's government expressed indignation at a proposed 10 percent tariff on Embraer aircraft and changes to sugar tariff conditions, accused Washington of trying to force changes on Pix and digital trade, and called the USTR's unilateral decision "an unacceptable action within the ongoing bilateral negotiation process" [32]. Brazil had already challenged earlier US tariffs at the WTO and is contesting the additional tariffs through the trade body [32][45]. Itamaraty said the government will evaluate the impacts and repercussions of the new proposed tariffs [46].

Public consultations on the proposed tariffs run through January, with hearings scheduled for January 25, 2027 [9][26][29]. The US-China trade truce, extended by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, expires January 10 [23]. The G20 summit in mid-December will provide a venue for affected governments to engage Washington directly [27].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.

Private companies, trade associations, and commercial actors.
international_org
Multilateral bodies and their representatives (UN agencies, IMF, IAEA, Red Cross, regional alliances).
judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.
media
Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.

Armed forces personnel, commanders, and defense ministries.
