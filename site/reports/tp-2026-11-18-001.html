US Strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island Followed by Drone Attacks on Bahrain and Kuwait as Conflict Zone Expands

Kuwait reports hostile drone interceptions as tanker fires burn in the Strait of Hormuz and Washington and Tehran trade accusations over an alleged dirty-bomb plot.

Kuwait's military announced its air defenses were confronting hostile drone attacks on November 18, with the General Staff saying any explosions heard were the result of air-defense interceptions [38][29]. Hours earlier, Bahrain's armed forces said they had "confronted, intercepted and destroyed a number of treacherous Iranian aerial attacks" [27], and the United States struck an Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps missile launch site on Iran's Qeshm Island [27][4]. The exchanges extended a confrontation that already included Iranian drone strikes on a US radar installation in Bahrain, tanker fires in the Strait of Hormuz, and a contested intelligence report alleging Iran was developing plans for a radiological attack on Washington.

The IRGC said its combat drones carried out a precision operation targeting a US-operated radar system at Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain, calling it retaliation for repeated violations of Iranian airspace by US military drones [12][27]. US Central Command spokesperson Tim Hawkins said two Iranian drones were shot down and US forces were not harmed, and Washington rejected Iran's claim of destroying an early-warning radar [31]. Researcher Oral Toğa of the Iran Studies Center argued the target selection followed a logic of "altyapıya karşı altyapı" (infrastructure for infrastructure), describing the strikes as measured signaling rather than a strategic shift [31].

The escalation extended along a parallel Yemen-Saudi front: Yemen's Houthi movement claimed drone strikes with "direct and precise hits" on two pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline, the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed a drone launched toward Riyadh, and Houthi-linked media reported four airstrikes on Sanaa's Bani al-Harith district, which the Houthi health ministry said injured at least nine people [4][24].

Ahmad Nafisi, deputy governor of Hormozgan Province, said the US strikes on Qeshm hit areas outside residential and commercial zones and caused no damage to vital infrastructure [4][21]. A police commander on Qeshm, Mojtaba Morovati, gave a different account, saying a drone strike on a family tourist camp in the island's Shour valley killed two men and one woman and wounded another, far from any military site [41]. Iran's energy minister, Abbas Aliabadi, said damage to a desalination facility had been fully remedied, while the CEO of Hormozgan Water and Wastewater Company, Abdolhamid Hamzehpour, said emergency operations were under way to supply water to 40,000 residents from alternative sources after the plant was destroyed [41]. Iraqi outlet Al-Sumaria, citing local sources, reported at least six injuries from the Qeshm strikes and said Iranian air and naval forces claimed to have retaliated with ballistic and cruise missiles and drones against the US destroyers involved in the attack [26].

Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei called the CNN report alleging an Iranian dirty-bomb or chemical-weapons plot against senior Trump administration officials a fabrication, writing on X that it amounted to "داستان‌سرایی" (storytelling) and "فضاسازی اهریمنی" (demonic framing) designed to justify attacks on Iran's civilian infrastructure [8]. Foreign Minister Seyed Abbas Araghchi dismissed the claims as "مزخرف محض" (pure nonsense), comparing them to Iraq-war-era propaganda and saying Israel's fingerprints were evident [20][27]. Iran's mission to the United Nations said Tehran "categorically rejects and unequivocally refutes the false and fabricated allegations" and regards the use of chemical weapons as illegitimate under any circumstances [2][11].

President Donald Trump, speaking at the ASEAN summit in Manila, said the United States takes the reported threat "very seriously because they happen to be crazy" and warned that Iran would "pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before" [9][6][15]. White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said the administration was "monitoring any and all potential threats, but at this time, there are no credible threats to the homeland" [9][10]. The CNN report cited seven unnamed US officials and presented no public evidence; some sources described the plans as detailed with specific locations and targets, while others characterized them as preserving options rather than an imminent attack [2][10]. The US attorney general, the director of national intelligence, and the CIA director urged Congress to renew a lapsed foreign surveillance law, warning that delay jeopardized American lives [23].

Independent analysts assessed the dirty-bomb threat as technically plausible but strategically unlikely. Talha Abdulrazaq of the University of Exeter said a dirty bomb's "primary effect would be psychological panic and hysteria" rather than mass casualties, and that any such attack would destroy Iran's remaining diplomatic credit [2]. Scott Roecker of the Nuclear Threat Initiative said dirty bombs are "really easy to make" from widely available radioactive sources [2]. Chris Kremidas-Courtney of the Euro-Atlantic Resilience Centre wrote that Iran likely has enough radioactive material but that a dirty bomb would be a "tool of last resort under extreme circumstances" and that direct infiltration of the US poses a high bar [2][16].

In the Strait of Hormuz, the IRGC reported that an oil supertanker and a gas carrier struck naval mines and exploded [21][33]. UK Maritime Trade Operations reported a tanker attacked twice north of Oman that caught fire, and that the crew of a tanker attacked north of the Omani port city of Khasab was unaccounted for [1][7]. Maritime security firm Marisks said there was no independent evidence of mines and characterized the IRGC's claim that the southern shipping lanes are "fully mined" as part of a campaign to frighten commercial shipping [34]. War-risk premiums for tanker transits have risen sharply, and marine underwriters absorbed another total loss from a sinking in the strait [34]. United Nations data recorded 134 confirmed maritime incidents and 25 seafarer deaths in the shipping crisis as of 11 November [35]. At a Security Council session requested by Bahrain, the GCC said member states had faced more than 7,000 Iranian missiles and drones since 28 February, while Iran's ambassador blamed unprovoked US-Israeli aggression he said had killed 5,000 Iranians [35].

Bahrain's Shura Council second deputy chair Jehad Al-Fadhel called the Iranian strikes on Bahraini civilian areas a "complete crime" [39]. Maria Khoury, former head of Bahrain's National Institution for Human Rights, wrote: "أي وجعٍ هذا الذي يطرق باب وطنٍ عرف بالسلام؟" (What pain is this that knocks on the door of a country known for peace?) [39]. Baghaei, for his part, cited US and Israeli strikes on water reservoirs and desalination facilities in Khomein, Haftkel, Qeshm, Nikshahr, Tis, and Jask as evidence that Washington was targeting civilian infrastructure [8].

Qatar called on all parties "to avoid any escalation that could undermine negotiation efforts" [5][6]. Araghchi met Oman's Sultan Haitham bin Tarik Al Said in Muscat, briefing him on Iran's diplomatic initiative and saying Iran defends its territorial integrity "with decisiveness and determination" [4]. Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir told Araghchi that sustained Pakistan-Iran engagement is needed for regional stability [7]. Russia's deputy MENA director Maxim Maksimov said Moscow could not exclude a force scenario if negotiations fail, noting three US carrier strike groups and two Marine expeditionary groups massed in the region [44].

The economic effects extended well beyond the Gulf. Iraqi Foreign Minister Fuad Hussein estimated Iraq's losses from the conflict at roughly $60 billion [19]. South Korea's trade minister Kim Jeong-gwan said "수급이 개선되고 있다는 것과 위기가 끝났다는 것은 전혀 다른 이야기" (improving supply is entirely different from the crisis being over) [42]. Fuel prices rose across East, Southern, and West Africa [45], and Greenpeace expert Nina Noelle called the Gulf pollution from tanker strikes "quite frankly an environmental disaster in the making" [47].

No attribution for the drone attacks on Kuwait has been publicly claimed or assigned [38][29]. Bahraini civil servant Abdulrahman Abdullah described the atmosphere: "Fear and anxiety rise as attacks increase … We hope things will go back to how they were" [27].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society
Non-state organizations representing collective interests (NGOs, human rights groups, trade unions, religious bodies).
government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.
industry
Private companies, trade associations, and commercial actors.
international_org
Multilateral bodies and their representatives (UN agencies, IMF, IAEA, Red Cross, regional alliances).
judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.
media
Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
military
Armed forces personnel, commanders, and defense ministries.
