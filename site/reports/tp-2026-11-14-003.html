US Envoys Say Europe Secures Role in Ukraine-Russia Peace Negotiations as EU Agrees on Joint Team

US envoys say Putin accepted European participation; the Kremlin conditions it on Europe proving peaceful intentions, while EU leaders settle on a five-nation plus institutions negotiating format.

US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff announced after meetings with Vladimir Putin in Moscow and Volodymyr Zelensky in Dublin that both Russia and Ukraine had agreed to expand the next round of peace negotiations to include European partners [1][3][4]. Kushner described the discussions as substantive and said European inclusion would allow the talks to address broader post-war European security arrangements alongside territorial issues [3][29]. Zelensky welcomed the move, saying Europe would have a voice in the negotiations [4][20]. The announcement followed a round of talks in Abu Dhabi that produced the largest prisoner exchange of the war but left core territorial disputes unresolved [9][40].

The Kremlin contradicted the US announcement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia would decide on an expanded format only after understanding Europe's "genuine intention and readiness to contribute to the peace process" [5][11][17]. Russian Foreign Ministry spokesperson Maria Zakharova said the topic of European participation was not discussed during the Moscow talks with Witkoff and Kushner [22][30]. Russian presidential aide Yuri Ushakov did not directly confirm the agreement, saying Europeans must decide whether they want peace or continued war [36][41]. Russian chief negotiator Vladimir Medinski questioned what exactly there would be to discuss with Europe [12], and former Russian President Dmitry Medvedev called EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas "stupid" and said the EU had not been invited to the talks [31]. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called the contradictory Russian signals the "usual game" [22].

At a Dublin summit, EU leaders resolved weeks of contention over representation by agreeing that EU institutions plus Germany, France, the UK, Italy and Poland would jointly represent Europe in the negotiations [2][9][16][19][22]. European Council President Antonio Costa said Europe must "speak with one clear and united voice" and that the arrangement was a "team effort" [2][16][19]. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni had warned that projecting a disunited Europe serves "the interests of those who want this war to continue" [6]. The agreement left institutional and national roles contested: Kallas asserted that she is "the one who is representing Europe in the foreign and security policy" [2][30], while national leaders including Macron were reportedly reluctant to cede France's seat to Brussels [13]. Kushner said that if Europe could identify one person to speak on its behalf, "that would be a bigger achievement than getting the Russians to include them in the negotiation" [2].

Whether a redesigned European security architecture should be on the agenda before or after a ceasefire divided the parties. Kushner said European inclusion would "allow us to address the broader post-war European security arrangements" [14][29], and Russian officials including Lavrov said Moscow wants to negotiate a broad European security framework before addressing the specifics of the war [5][40]. Kallas warned that engaging Russia's security-architecture demands before a ceasefire means walking into "traps that Russia is setting us" by diverting attention from the absence of a ceasefire [11][14][29]. Estonia's Prime Minister Kristen Michal said debating Europe's security architecture before peace is "un piège mental" (a mental trap) set by Putin [39], and Merz said Europe does not need to discuss a new security architecture because "it already exists" [29][30]. Medinski framed the task as creating "un système de sécurité global qui deviendrait une nouvelle Yalta et Potsdam moderne" (a comprehensive security system that would become a modern Yalta and Potsdam) [39].

Russia's territorial and security demands remained unchanged. Putin said Russia's position on the war and its territorial demand had not changed [4], and stated that "for us, there is only one line of demarcation: the line of the state border" [46]. Bild reported that Russian negotiators tabled demands including Finland leaving NATO, a ban on NATO bases in the Baltics, Ukraine ceding the rest of Donbas, demilitarized Odesa and Mykolaiv, and a federal Moldova [11][43], which the Kremlin denied [44]. Putin warned that European troops deployed in Ukraine after a peace deal would be "a legitimate target" and that war would resume immediately [6][32]. The reported demands were rejected by frontline states. Finnish Defence Minister Antti Häkkänen said "these matters are not up for negotiation" [11], and Finnish parliamentary committee chair Johannes Koskinen said any such demand "will go straight into the bin" [11]. Lithuania's Foreign Minister Kęstutis Budrys said Lithuania would block any agreement giving Russia power over NATO deployments [11]. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said any peace deal must preserve Ukraine's sovereignty and not alter Eastern Europe's security architecture [9]. Moldovan government spokesman Dumitru Ciorici said Moscow raises "scare scenarios" as Moldova advances toward the EU [41], and former Constitutional Court president Alexandru Tănase compared the reported demands to the 2003 Kozak Memorandum and said Moldova's future cannot be decided without its participation [41].

European leaders expressed concern that Washington could strike a deal over their heads. Merz urged the US to avoid surprises in Trump's expected talks with Putin, saying "please, no surprises" and calling for continued coordination with "Team Europe" and "Team Ukraine" [2][11][30]. Macron said "no decision concerning the security of Europe can be made without the Europeans" [20]. Former Estonian NATO ambassador Jüri Luik faulted Washington for opening the door to Russian demands about Europe's security, saying it was troubling that "Russia has been put in a position where it can make demands and state its positions at all" [44]. Witkoff had praised Putin as a "humanitarian" [24], and Trump remarked that Ukraine might "end up with a little bit different shape" after the war [24].

Within the Dublin summit, Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico opposed any further financial and military support for Ukraine [6], while other leaders pressed for continued backing. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen announced the EU is preparing a 22nd sanctions package against Russia and a proposal to cover Ukraine's 2027 financial needs, with European Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis saying Brussels would propose €45 billion for Kyiv next year [14][27]. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen urged leaders to put contributions on the table to help Ukraine "get them through a tough, tough winter" [11]. Ukrainian civil society figures published a joint statement setting out eight demands to negotiators, including no recognition of occupation and real security guarantees [42].

A follow-up round of talks is already scheduled [1], with Zelensky saying the next meeting could be hosted by the UAE or Switzerland [21]. Ushakov said a Putin-Trump meeting at the APEC summit in Shenzhen is being worked on [32]. Costa said Russia's continued attacks on civilians cast doubt on its readiness for peace and that the coming weeks are a decisive test [16].

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
