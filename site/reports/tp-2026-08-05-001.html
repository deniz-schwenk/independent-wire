Washington asserts direct negotiations are underway while Iran denies any US talks and reports progress only with Oman on strait management.

President Donald Trump declared that ongoing talks with Iran represent Tehran's "last chance" to reach a deal before facing "decapitation," while Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) announced it had shot down a US MQ-9 Reaper drone over the Strait of Hormuz [1][4][7][33][34]. Trump stated that negotiations were "going on right now" at Iran's request and with backing from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar, outlining a two-phase plan: first reopening the Strait of Hormuz, then addressing denuclearization [4][12][25]. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baqaei categorically denied that any negotiations with the United States were taking place, stating that Iran's discussions were limited to Oman and focused on securing safe passage through the strait [3][5][12].

The contradiction over whether US-Iran talks exist at all runs through the diplomatic landscape. Trump told Fox News that Iran would suffer "a very hard blow" if it backed out of negotiations again, and accused Iranian leadership of being "unbelievably duplicitous" for publicly denying the talks [4][28][30]. Baqaei stated that Iran has no plans to send or receive delegations and that engagement is confined to technical and political negotiations with Muscat [15][19][24]. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said a deal to reopen the strait could be reached "today or tomorrow" [22][29], though Dawn reported him citing a Tuesday-or-Wednesday timeframe [8]; Secretary of State Marco Rubio reported progress without finality [8]. Qatar's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Majed al-Ansari described mediation as reaching "very progressive stages" with a draft agreement circulating between parties [8][21][29].

Iranian officials described the Oman negotiations as close to completion around an arrangement that would give Tehran effective control over strait passage. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the talks with Muscat were "nearing completion and in their final stages," centered on a route respecting the sovereignty of both countries [3][5]. Baqaei told state television that the arrangement would be "neither the northern route nor the southern route — but one that respects the sovereign rights of both sides" [28]. Saeed Ajourlou, a member of Iran's negotiation committee, stated that the strait "will not return to its pre-war status" and that Iran would be the controlling party [42]. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi demanded that ships take a northern route close to Iran and rejected any third-country de-mining role [12]. The Guardian, however, reported that Oman had resisted aspects of the proposed arrangement over fears it could become permanent, and described splits between Iran's foreign ministry and regime hardliners over its terms [29].

Trump asserted that the US Navy "already completely controls" the Strait of Hormuz and that nothing reaches Iran without US permission until a deal or surrender is achieved [5][17][25]. US Central Command (CENTCOM) stated that its forces continue to "strictly enforce" the blockade against Iran, having redirected 44 commercial vessels, disabled two, and boarded two [5][25]. Mohsen Rezaee, an adviser to Iran's Supreme Leader, warned that US warships and bases would face "grave danger" if the blockade continues and that Iran would "never permit the opening of a second corridor" in the strait [10][25][31]. According to Iran's Press TV, the IRGC's claim of downing the MQ-9 drone was indirectly corroborated by US Air Force Lieutenant General David Tabor, who reportedly acknowledged the urgent need to replenish the MQ-9 fleet depleted by Iranian air defenses [34].

Internal US military deliberations reported by Turkish media revealed doubts about achieving Trump's objectives through air power alone. Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff General Dan Caine stated that "air power has its limits," and a CENTCOM officer requested "creative and unconventional" ways to pressure Iran [13]. The report noted that ground options were being considered despite political risks and ammunition stockpile concerns [13]. Editorials in Pakistan's Dawn and Spain's El País characterized Trump's alternating threats and diplomatic optimism as policy without a coherent exit strategy, with Dawn describing Operation "Epic Fury" as "an epic failure" that left Iran retaining its nuclear capabilities and increasing control over the strait [11][26].

The crisis has produced divergent assessments of the global economic stakes. Pimco representative Greg Sharenow stated that the global market is "without a doubt" facing a supply deficit [27]. Michael Knights of the Washington Institute said Iran's advantage is that "they can hurt the regional states and the global economy" [8][25]. Associate Professor Ali Oguz Dirioz said that even if the Hormuz crisis ended, "countries will continue looking for more resilient transport networks instead of relying on a single maritime route" [5]. Oil prices dropped after Trump's announcement and Bessent's optimism, with Brent crude falling over 4 percent, though The Guardian reported a roughly 2 percent decline tied to a separate US-Qatar progress announcement [4][27][29].

Gulf states are pursuing alternative export routes to reduce dependence on the strait. UAE Minister of State for Foreign Trade Thani bin Ahmed Al Zeyoudi said the UAE is moving toward "zero dependence" on Hormuz [40]. Saudi Aramco is analyzing alternatives including expanding the capacity of its east-west pipeline to Yanbu port, with CEO Amin Nasser stating the pipeline played a "pivotal role" in maintaining supply resilience [27][40]. A Kuwaiti analysis noted that while the cost of bypass infrastructure is high, the cost of its absence is far greater, particularly for Kuwait, which faces extreme vulnerability [40]. The US government's Development Finance Corporation (DFC) launched a war-risk reinsurance program for oil tankers after commercial insurers abandoned the region, with DFC chief Ben Black framing it as a rescue of a market that had walked away [37]. Policy fellow Clemence Landers called the program a "profound departure" from normal practice that subsidizes private investors, and US Representative Joaquin Castro objected that it would protect oil shipments benefiting China [37].

The economic effects extend beyond the Gulf. BBC Swahili reported rising fuel and transport costs across Nigeria, Egypt, South Africa, Tanzania, and Kenya, with security researcher Timothy Walker noting that longer shipping routes put pressure on port infrastructure [47]. Economist Dumebi Oluwole from Lagos said that when petrol prices rise globally, transport costs increase everywhere [47]. Jacques Moolman, president of the Cape Chamber of Commerce, cited infrastructure constraints despite potential business opportunities in storage and ship repair [47].

The war has also undermined nuclear verification capabilities. UN Under-Secretary-General for Political Affairs Rosemary DiCarlo stated that the IAEA has lost continuity of knowledge on Iran's nuclear program and that this knowledge cannot be restored [46]. Lecturer Andrew Thomas argued that Iran's chokehold on global oil flows gives it a potent non-nuclear deterrent, potentially reducing the perceived need for a weapons program, while noting that some hardliners may be more receptive to weaponization since "knowledge and capabilities cannot be bombed away" [6]. Iran's Assembly of Experts warned that "hope for understanding and agreement with this bullying regime will lead nowhere" [12].

UN Deputy Spokesperson Farhan Haq called for an immediate cessation of hostilities, warning that the conflict spreading to more countries "could get us into a much worse situation" [14]. Amnesty International's Heba Morayef condemned Iran's wave of executions as a tool to "punish and suppress dissent and project an image of strength" amid the conflict [14]. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian stated that "we defend our borders, but we do not seek to expand the war" [23]. A cargo vessel was struck near the strait with one seafarer reported missing [23], and a regional diplomat told The Guardian that "we may be on the brink of a regional war because there is a big faction in Iran that does not want a deal under any circumstances" [29].

Bessent and Qatari mediators continue to predict a deal within days, while Iran insists its only negotiations are with Oman and its military leadership vows retaliation against any continued US blockade [22][24][31]. Oil markets remain volatile, with transit through the strait sharply reduced and commercial insurers requiring government backing to operate in the war zone [25][37][38].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society

government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.

international_org

judiciary

legislature
Parliament, Congress, or equivalent body. Kept separate from “government” because legislatures often hold positions that differ from their own executive branch.

Journalists, editorial boards, and outlets quoted for their position or analysis, not as sources of factual reporting.
