Trump Proposes New Tariffs on China and 13 Trade Partners One Week After Xi Summit

The package cites structural overcapacity; China calls the rationale a pretext, partners deny the accusation, and Thailand prepares exemption talks while Mexico remains outside the round.

One week after the Trump-Xi summit, the United States Trade Representative proposed new Section 301 tariffs on China and 13 other trading partners — 14 economies in all — with Mexico excluded from this round but remaining under consideration, and Thailand preparing to negotiate exemptions with US officials [3][10][20][45]. The proposed duties range from 15% to 40%, with lower ceilings for partners that have concluded bilateral trade agreements and steeper rates for those without [3][7]. China faces a 20% rate that could reach 40% if the trade truce expires, Vietnam faces up to 35% without a deal, and India faces 25% or 17% with an agreement [3][13].

USTR Jamieson Greer said the investigation found that trading partners' industrial strategies — built on subsidies, wage suppression, and non-market inputs — have created structural excess capacity that erodes America's manufacturing base [3][21]. Greer said the tariffs would "level the playing field for American workers" [25]. White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said the administration was stopping American workers and businesses from being "ripped off by lopsided trade agreements" [27]. The United Steelworkers union called the tariff actions "long overdue" [27].

China's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called the so-called "overcapacity" a "false issue" and said China opposes using it as an excuse for political manipulation [3][7]. Commerce Ministry spokesperson He Yadong called the Section 301 investigation "a typical act of unilateralism" that violates WTO rules and warned the tariffs could raise global inflation, disrupt supply chains, and create long-term systemic risks [14][28]. He said China reserves the right to take necessary measures [5].

Japan, the EU, Singapore, India, and Malaysia denied having structural overcapacity or market-distorting policies, while South Korean trade authorities said they disagreed with parts of the report's findings [15][20]. Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara said Japan has "no non-market policy practices or structural excess production capacity" and called the proposed measure "regrettable" [8][15]. The European Commission said it "strongly rejects the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity" and demanded Washington honor the agreed 15% tariff ceiling [7][33]. EU spokesperson Olof Gill said the EU and US share concerns about state-driven overcapacity concentrated in third countries but regretted the lack of joint action [8][12]. India's commerce ministry said India has no structural excess capacity in any sector and that trade concerns should be resolved in ongoing negotiations [13][16]. Singapore said it was "very disappointed" and denied protecting its domestic market or subsidizing manufacturing [8][12].

Brazil's president's office called the measures "unacceptable" and said Brazil "will not yield to this type of threat" while bilateral talks are ongoing [8][12]. The office did not rule out retaliatory measures, with sugar and aircraft duties flagged as flashpoints, and ruled out a direct leader-to-leader call for now [12][43].

Analysts read the rate structure as a deliberate incentive scheme. Peter Draper of the University of Adelaide said the higher rates for partners without reciprocal agreements incentivize those countries to conclude deals with the US [6]. Madeline Chalecki of the Atlantic Council said "the threat of additional tariffs may be a better negotiating chip than the duties themselves" [3]. Vietnam pledged to complete a trade deal and cooperate with the overcapacity investigation [6][12]. Thailand's government prepared to negotiate exemptions for 15 additional product categories [45]. Cambodia announced tariff cuts on US goods and requested negotiations [46]. Singapore's Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong said any final agreement would involve "compromises" and "trade-offs" [26].

Economists and US lawmakers warned that American households would bear the cost. Yale University's Budget Lab estimated tariffs raise US household costs by $900 annually [27]. Deborah Elms of the Hinrich Foundation said "it's not the exporters that pay these prices, it's actually Americans who pay these prices" and warned of higher inflation [6]. US Representative Don Bacon called tariffs "a tax on American consumers" and urged lawmakers to reclaim authority over import duties [27]. Incoming House Majority Leader Katherine Clark said Republicans "have learned nothing" from voter anger over costs [27]. Senator Ron Wyden called the tariffs "a blatant attempt to reconstruct illegal global tariffs" and vowed to use every tool to end what he described as tariff chaos [3][12].

The announcement follows the US Supreme Court's ruling that Trump's April 2025 "Liberation Day" tariffs were illegal [4][7]. Georgetown law professor Gregory Shaffer said Trump is trying to "set tariffs globally" without Congress [3]. ING economist Inga Fechner wrote that "while the legal justification for tariffs keeps changing, US protectionism is becoming increasingly difficult to unwind" [3]. Josh Lipsky of the Atlantic Council described the announcement as an attempt to set up a "tariff web — one meant to stick past this administration" [3].

Several analysts framed the package as an instrument of US-China strategic competition. Capital Economics economist Mark Williams said if a trade war resumes, the new measures give the US "a certain arsenal of tools" and that focusing on China in reports on third countries echoes Trump's attempts to pressure other states to distance themselves from Beijing [22]. Eurasia Group's Dan Wang said tariff escalation before major summits "has become a pattern" and said the new measures would not derail US-China relations [12]. Derek Scissors of the American Enterprise Institute said the China rates are too weak, "barely raises existing tariffs on China and will make no difference to the country's trade behaviour" [12].

Indian industry associations described concrete sectoral exposure. The Chamber of Trading Industry warned that without retaliatory measures, export markets for pharmaceuticals, electronics, gems and jewellery, leather, metals, and marine products would suffer and 500,000 livelihoods would be at risk [16]. Indian MSMEs reported order uncertainty clouding the export outlook [34].

The USTR will accept public comments through January 5 and hold hearings from January 25, with implementation possible as early as January 2027 [21][13]. Thailand is preparing exemption negotiations with US officials, while Mexico — excluded from this round — remains under consideration [10][45]. Lianhe Zaobao reported that analysts still expect the US-China trade truce to be extended despite the tariff proposal [35].

About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”
