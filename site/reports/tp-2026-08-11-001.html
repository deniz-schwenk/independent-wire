Nearly all evaluative and intensifying language in the article is properly attributed to named sources or officials. One borderline instance involves the article's use of 'severe economic damage' in its own voice to describe the blockade's impact on Iran, though supporting figures follow in the same passage. The word 'framing' applied to Trump's compensation demands could be read as subtly editorial, though it is also standard journalistic usage.

President Donald Trump stated that the US Navy has mine-swept the entire Strait of Hormuz and now fully controls the waterway, asserting that the strait is open and that any remaining blockade targets only Iran-bound shipping [8][14]. Iranian officials rejected that characterization, conditioning any reopening on the United States ending its naval blockade, lifting sanctions, freeing frozen assets, and paying compensation [1][2][13]. An Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps spokesman described the strait as "a theatre of war for us and not just a waterway" and vowed to maintain the closure until all Iranian conditions are met [11]. Ayatollah Amoli Larijani, head of Iran's Expediency Council, declared that Iran will defend its position in the strait at any cost, asserting Tehran's sovereignty over the waterway [12].

Iran's conditions comprise six demands articulated by officials including Supreme National Security Council secretary Bagher Zolghadr and Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi: an end to US threats and aggression, lifting the naval blockade, sanctions relief, release of frozen assets, compensation for war damage, and what Tehran describes as corrected US behavior [3][5][7][15]. Araqchi stated that an agreement with Oman on new shipping lanes is in its final stages but that the strait will not reopen unless US conditions are met [5]. US officials confirmed that Iran and Oman could soon reach an agreement and that Washington would lift its blockade of Iranian ports once commercial shipping resumed without impediments [1][5]. Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei denied direct negotiations with the US, saying contacts are limited to messages via intermediaries [13].

Trump described his approach as low-key and semi-negotiating, saying the US is watching Iran's inflation and depleted budget and that "everything works out like a chess game" [3][5][7][11]. Trump also stated that the US will demand compensation from Iran for war casualties and for people killed during protests over the past 50 years, framing it as a response to Tehran's own compensation demands [9][10]. US officials reported that Trump has privately indicated he could abandon efforts to secure a nuclear agreement with Iran in return for reopening the strait, prioritizing oil movement while maintaining forces in the region [6].

Analysts offered competing readings of Iran's demands. Former US Secretary of Defense Mark Esper said the six conditions show Tehran's leadership is increasingly confident in its position thanks to its leverage over the strait, and former NATO commander James Stavridis said the demands are narrowing the US administration's options [7]. Center for Strategic and International Studies director Mona Yacoubian said Iran's demands are making it increasingly difficult for the US president to create a smooth exit from the conflict [7]. Eurasia Group analyst Gregory Brew said Iran believes Trump wants to withdraw and is only seeking to reopen the strait, so Tehran has decided to play hardball [7]. Bourse & Bazaar Foundation founder Esfandyar Batmanghelidj said the level of mistrust is so great that Tehran wants to secure promised economic relief before relinquishing even partial control of the strait [7]. Quincy Institute analyst Trita Parsi offered a contrasting view, stating that Iran's public demands are exaggerated and likely a negotiating tactic to slow the process [15].

The blockade is inflicting severe economic damage on Iran's population, with reporting describing 90 percent inflation, food price spikes, and rent increases [29]. Economic researcher Siamak Javadi warned that a prolonged blockade risks hyperinflation and even famine in worst-case scenarios, while economist Ahmad Alavi said the economy retains some resilience and collapse is not inevitable [16]. Kpler senior oil analyst Homayoun Falakshahi stated that there is no escape from the Strait of Hormuz and that alternative routes cannot compensate for the loss of southern ports [16]. A Baker Institute study co-authored by Jim Krane and Justin Alexander found that initial export losses for Gulf neighbors averaged nearly $2 billion per day, with Kuwait, Qatar, and Bahrain the hardest hit and Oman identified as a beneficiary [21].

Gulf states are accelerating pipeline projects to bypass the strait, including the UAE's West-East Pipeline and Saudi Arabia's East-West expansion [26]. King's College London associate professor David B. Roberts said alternative routes are far from sufficient, and H.A. Hellyer of the Royal United Services Institute said Gulf states are more determined than ever to reduce reliance on Hormuz but will not fully replace it [17]. Senior analyst Hazir Ezekiel Dhu al-Hisham warned that alternative routes may replace one dependency with another, giving transit states like Turkey more leverage [17].

Maritime industry analysts emphasized that operational uncertainty is deterring shipping from resuming transit. EOS Risk Group analyst Martin Kelly said an extremely brave captain is needed to transit the strait given the current security environment, and Windward Maritime Intelligence analyst Michelle Wiese Bockmann noted that many captains remember the April incident where Iran declared the strait open and then closed it the next day [18]. Kpler senior oil market analyst Navin Das said the first sign of reopening will be a mass outflow of vessels, but owners are adopting a wait-and-see approach [18]. International Maritime Organization Secretary-General Arsenio Dominguez stated that demining is a critical first step to restoring shipping to pre-war levels, and International Association of Independent Tanker Owners representative Phillip Belcher said uncertainty about mines remains a serious concern [18].

US-based legal analysts argued that Iran's attacks on civilian vessels and potential mining violate international law of the sea and the law of armed conflict [22]. Legal analyst Mark Nevitt wrote that allowing Iran to charge ships for transit would set a precedent that could be exploited by other powers, including China in the South China Sea and Taiwan Strait [23]. The Institute for China-America Studies examined the legal erosion of transit passage rights, arguing that the danger is not formal closure but de facto obstruction through risk, cost, and interference [24].

Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement as a counterweight to Iran's strait leverage [1]. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said the alliance is not directed against Iran or any other country, and Pakistan's foreign ministry said the agreement is intended to strengthen collective deterrence [5][11]. A regional official stated that Iran would likely test the agreement through a proxy such as the Houthis or Iran-aligned Iraqi militias [1].

The disruption is causing global economic harm beyond oil prices. Reporting from Africa documented fuel shortages and price spikes across Kenya, Somalia, Nigeria, Mali, Mozambique, Ethiopia, and Cape Verde [19]. Analysis from Pakistan highlighted rising prices for fertilizers, helium for microchips, petrochemicals for medicine, and sulfur for batteries [20]. Council on Foreign Relations senior fellow Prashant Yadav warned that prolonged helium shortages could increase MRI costs [20]. In Italy, oil consumption dropped 4 percent, with ENEA analyst Francesco Gracceva attributing the reduction mainly to a drop in petrochemicals rather than transport fuel [27].

Chinese and Korean analyses framed the crisis as manageable for large importers. Columbia University scholar Erica Downs said China's strategic and commercial reserves may be sufficient to last up to six months even if Middle East supply is completely cut off [25]. The Korea Energy Economics Institute published a policy portfolio for South Korea's energy security response, including price caps, stockpile releases, and export restrictions [28].

Oman-mediated technical talks on new shipping lanes and maritime security mechanisms are in their final stages, according to Iranian and US officials [1][5][11]. Oman's foreign ministry condemned repeated attacks on shipping and warned against actions jeopardizing progress on navigation arrangements [11]. The appointment of Mohsen Rezaei as secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council signals a rebalancing toward the security establishment, with Dawn reporting that Rezaei is likely to insist that any operational reopening produce demonstrable gains for Iran while keeping the military dimension available as leverage [4].

However, 'framing' is widely used in journalism as a neutral descriptor for how a speaker presents an argument. About these labels

Not every tag needs a definition — those listed below cover the full vocabulary used across the dossier.

Divergence types

factual
Sources disagree on a verifiable fact: a date, number, name, or whether something happened.
framing
Sources describe the same event using different language or implied meaning. Example: one outlet calls a payment “compensation,” another calls it “sanctions relief.”
omission
One or more sources report something that other sources leave out entirely.
emphasis
Sources cover the same event but give different aspects different weight or prominence. Example: one outlet leads with casualty figures; another treats them as a footnote to the political negotiations.

Bias issues

evaluative_adjective
A descriptive word that signals the writer’s judgment rather than a neutral fact. Examples: “staggering,” “sharp,” “dramatic.”
intensifier
A word that amplifies a statement without adding information. Examples: “very,” “extremely,” “deeply.”
loaded_term
Vocabulary carrying strong political or emotional connotations that a more neutral word would avoid. Examples: “regime” vs. “government,” “crackdown” vs. “enforcement.”
hedging
Phrases that soften or obscure a claim, making attribution less clear. Examples: “some say,” “allegedly,” “reportedly.”

Stakeholder types

academia
Researchers, professors, think tanks, and university-based experts.
affected_community
People directly impacted by the events themselves — civilians, displaced persons, local populations. Voices from within the group, not their spokespersons.
civil_society
Non-state organizations representing collective interests (NGOs, human rights groups, trade unions, religious bodies).
government
Executive branch officials, ministries, heads of state, and their spokespersons.
industry
Private companies, trade associations, and commercial actors.
international_org
Multilateral bodies and their representatives (UN agencies, IMF, IAEA, Red Cross, regional alliances).
judiciary
Judges, courts, prosecutors, and legal bodies acting in their official capacity.
