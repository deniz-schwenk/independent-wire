Ceuta Death Toll Reaches 77 as EU Expresses Solidarity with Spain Amid Persistent Political Divisions

EU ministers declare the resilience test passed, while disputes over Morocco's role, pushback legality, and return hub models continue across member states.

The death toll from the mass crossing into Spain's enclave of Ceuta has risen to 77, Spanish authorities confirmed, as EU interior ministers formally expressed solidarity with Madrid after an emergency meeting in Brussels [2][6][20]. Ceuta's own authorities report 88 deaths, with 11 additional bodies found on the Moroccan side, while German public broadcaster Tagesschau reports 84 deaths — discrepancies that remain unresolved [10][15]. European Commissioner Magnus Brunner stated that Europe's resilience had been tested and the test was passed, with the Schengen area never compromised [20][26]. The European Commission offered Spain emergency assistance to strengthen the Ceuta border [16].

The EU's expression of solidarity followed an initial phase in which 22 member states signed a letter criticizing Spain's migration policy and Italy unilaterally suspended Schengen travel with Spain [9][15][21]. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni described the uncontrolled immigration as a security threat to Europe [2][30], and Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babiš urged a temporary suspension of Spain's Schengen membership [2]. French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez stated that Spain would not be excluded from the Schengen area and that Schengen was "n'était pas le problème" (not the problem) but "plutôt la solution" (rather the solution) [21][20].

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez accused some European governments of attacking Spain driven by "prejudice, fake news, ignorance or political interest" [18][30]. Foreign Minister José Manuel Albares denounced what he called a "synchronised reactionary international effort" that spread lies about the integrity of the Schengen area [23]. Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska stated that approximately 70,000 of the 72,000 migrants who entered Ceuta have been returned to Morocco and that the Schengen area was never compromised [2][6][20].

The question of Morocco's role remains contested. Ceuta president Juan Jesús Vivas described the crossing as "an atrocity" and an attack on Spain's territorial integrity that was, if not orchestrated, at least encouraged or permitted by Morocco [23]. Defense Minister Margarita Robles asked the Moroccan government to fully investigate what happened [23]. Migration researcher Jochen Oltmer stated that "such a border crossing could never have taken place if the Moroccan side hadn't looked the other way" [4]. A migrant identified as Ashraf recounted that Moroccan police were pushing people toward the water and telling them to swim [23].

Morocco rejected responsibility. A senior Moroccan government official argued that the number of migrants swelled to a point where security forces could no longer hold them back, and stated that it is not Morocco's responsibility alone to manage the problem [17]. Interior ministry spokesperson Rachid El Khalfi attributed the crisis to "the malicious exploitation of the digital space" and misinterpretations of a Spanish Supreme Court ruling [4]. A senior Moroccan official accused Spain of ignoring warnings that the court ruling, which prevents immediate returns of migrants arriving by sea, would cause border problems [32][17]. Isabelle Werenfels of the German Institute for International and Security Affairs cautioned that Morocco would risk too much by deliberately orchestrating such a surge, though it may have wanted to send a political signal [4]. Analyst Paul Melly of Chatham House linked the crisis to Sánchez's recent visit to Algeria, describing it as a diplomatic reparation for Spain's 2022 decision to support Morocco's Western Sahara autonomy plan [15].

Spain's recent regularization of approximately 500,000 undocumented migrants drew criticism from EU partners. Commissioner Brunner said the regularization "no es una buena señal" (is not a good signal) for Europe, while acknowledging that no direct link had been established between the policy and the Ceuta influx [6][9]. Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani criticized Spain for not consulting other EU countries on the regularization [6]. Migration expert Gerald Knaus disputed the connection, stating that after previous Spanish regularizations, irregular arrivals never increased [26]. Michele LeVoy, director of Picum, argued that the harsh response from member states was likely driven by fears of secondary movement rather than a proven pull effect [18].

Human rights organizations raised concerns about Spain's rapid returns. Human Rights Watch documented allegations of mass pushbacks without individual assessments by Spanish forces and excessive force by both Spanish and Moroccan authorities [29]. The Spanish Commission for Refugee Aid warned that sub-Saharan migrants are "las víctimas más desprotegidas" (the most unprotected victims), facing expulsions without legal guarantees [24]. ActionAid denounced the return of minors to remote areas of Morocco without safe return guarantees [44]. Legal scholar Constantin Hruschka, professor of European and Migration Law, argued that Spain's broad interpretation of the European Court of Human Rights ruling to justify pushbacks violates the European Convention on Human Rights [46]. Migrants described poor conditions on Ceuta's beaches, with a migrant named Kamal reporting no food, nowhere to sleep, and no blankets [2][30]. A Senegalese migrant named Omar stated: "We are able-bodied, we have a lot to contribute. We have come to work, not to beg" [27]. A Sudanese migrant named Mohamed Ahmed stated: "I'm not going back. I'd rather die trying here" [28].

Italy's government pushed to generalize the Italy-Albania migration hub model, with Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi proposing a European network of return hubs in third countries financed by EU funds [14][34]. The Albania centers have processed only 685 migrants since 2025 at an estimated cost of 495,000 euros per repatriated person, according to research institute Idos and NGO Tavolo Asilo e Immigrazione [14]. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called for stronger EU borders, including "vigilant monitoring and the use of physical barriers where needed" [18][7]. EPP leader Manfred Weber argued that Frontex should take command of national borders in exceptional crisis situations, a proposal opposed by SPD MEP René Repasi, who stated that Frontex is not accountable to the European Parliament and that public power without parliamentary oversight is illegitimate [21][26]. Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis wrote in a Politico opinion piece that the EU should allow for the temporary suspension of asylum registrations during sudden mass arrivals [3].

Analysts identified political exploitation of the crisis. Camille Le Coz, director of the Migration Policy Institute Europe, described Italy's Schengen suspension as "pure political theatre" and said far-right groups used images from Ceuta to portray Europe as "under attack" [7]. Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha stated that a Russian propaganda campaign using pictures from Ceuta had been detected across Europe to sow destabilization [23]. In Ceuta, far-right politicians Alvise Pérez and Santiago Abascal, who called the crossing an "invasión" (invasion), faced counter-protests from residents [5].

Ceuta residents largely blamed government failures rather than the migrants. Waiter Mohamad Abdelkader Driss said: "Los jóvenes no tienen la culpa" (The young people are not to blame), placing responsibility with "los grandes y aparte los que mandan" (the powerful and those in charge) [5]. Student Noor Ahmed denounced outside protesters as wanting to "aprovechar la crisis humanitaria" (exploit the humanitarian crisis) [5]. Restaurant owner Gema Molina Parra warned that without central government action, the influx would repeat [15].

Families of the missing continue to search for loved ones. The Moroccan Association for Human Rights reported families waiting day and night at the border for information on missing relatives [25]. Spain announced a 25 million euro special budget for the care of unaccompanied minors in Ceuta [6][11]. Between 3,000 and 5,000 migrants remain in the enclave, according to Ceuta's president [32]. Spain's other North African enclave, Melilla, also experienced irregular crossings, with 1,323 entries reported and 1,063 people returned to Morocco [6].
